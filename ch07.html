7  Externalities

7.1 Definitions

Externality. A cost or a benefit, arising from producing or consuming a good, that lands on someone who is not a party to the decision. That someone is called a bystander. The bystander neither pays for the effect nor is paid for it, through any price or contract.

Consider the market for firewood in a valley town, where \(Q^{d} = 100 - 10P\) and \(Q^{s} = 10P - 20\), and \(Q\) is measured in hundreds of bundles per week. Every bundle that is burned sends smoke onto households that bought no wood, and we suppose that the smoke from each bundle does $2.00 of harm to them.

Social cost. The private cost plus the external cost. Put another way, it is what one more unit costs society once the bystanders are included.

For example, at 4,000 bundles a week the marginal bundle of firewood has a private cost of $6.00. Add the $2.00 of harm from its smoke, and the social cost of that bundle is $8.00.

Deadweight loss. The total surplus that is lost to an inefficient outcome, most often because the quantity traded is not the quantity that maximizes total surplus. It is value destroyed either by trading too much or by trading too little, and nobody collects it.

The firewood market trades 4,000 bundles a week, but the quantity that maximizes total surplus is 3,000. Each of the 1,000 bundles in between costs society more than it is worth to its buyer, and the value destroyed adds up to $1,000 per week.

Internalizing an externality. Changing incentives so that the parties to a trade face the full social cost of that trade, or its full social value.

Suppose the firewood seller has to pay the $2.00 of harm on every bundle. Then the seller of bundle 4,000 faces the full $8.00 that the bundle costs society, and not only the $6.00 that it costs to produce.

Corrective tax. A tax on each unit, set equal to the external cost that the unit imposes. It lifts the curve that sellers respond to by exactly the amount by which that curve fell short of social cost.

A corrective tax is also called a Pigovian tax, and for firewood it is $2.00 a bundle. With the tax in place, buyers pay $7 a bundle, sellers keep $5, and the quantity falls from 4,000 bundles a week to 3,000.

Social value. Private value, meaning the buyer’s willingness to pay, plus the external benefit. In other words, it is what one more unit is worth to society when the bystanders are counted.

Consider a bike-share system in a congested city, where \(Q^{d} = 80 - 10P\) and \(Q^{s} = 10P - 20\), and \(Q\) is measured in hundreds of rides per day, and suppose each ride is worth $2.00 to the drivers who are still on the road. At 3,000 rides a day the marginal ride is worth $5.00 to its rider, so its social value is $7.00.

Tradable pollution permits. Each permit allows its holder to discharge one unit. The number of permits issued is equal to the cap, and firms may buy and sell them.

Suppose two factories each discharge 80 tons a month into the same river, and the town wants the total brought down to 100 tons. The town issues 100 permits, and a factory that cuts a ton can sell the permit this frees up to the other factory.

Coase theorem. If the affected parties can bargain at no cost, they will reach the efficient outcome by themselves, whichever side the law favors to begin with.

For example, suppose live music is worth $400 a week to a café, and costs the bookshop next door $600 a week in lost sales. The bookshop can offer the café $500 to keep quiet, and that leaves each of them $100 better off, even though the law gives the café the right to play.

7.2 Worked examples

The firewood market before the smoke is counted. \[100 - 10P = 10P - 20 \;\Longrightarrow\; 120 = 20P \;\Longrightarrow\; P^{*} = 6 ,\] and \(Q^{*} = 100 - 10(6) = 40\), or 4,000 bundles a week. As a check, substitute the price into supply instead: \(10(6) - 20 = 40\). The two routes give the same answer, and that agreement confirms it.

What a bundle is worth and what it costs society.

\(Q\) (00s) Worth to buyer Private cost Social cost Society’s gain
20 \(\frac{100-20}{10} = 8.00\) \(\frac{20+20}{10} = 4.00\) \(4.00 + 2 = 6.00\) \(+2.00\)
30 \(\frac{100-30}{10} = 7.00\) \(\frac{30+20}{10} = 5.00\) \(5.00 + 2 = 7.00\) \(\phantom{+}0.00\)
40 \(\frac{100-40}{10} = 6.00\) \(\frac{40+20}{10} = 6.00\) \(6.00 + 2 = 8.00\) \(-2.00\)

Start at 2,000 bundles. The marginal bundle is worth $8.00 and costs society $6.00, so burning it leaves the valley $2.00 better off. At 3,000 bundles, the value and the social cost are both $7.00, so the gain has run out. At 4,000, which is where the market settles, the bundle is worth $6.00 but costs society $8.00. So burning that bundle leaves the valley $2.00 worse off.

Figure 7.1 shows the two quantities.

The deadweight loss from the smoke. The loss on the marginal bundle grows steadily. It is $0.00 at 3,000 bundles, where value still equaled social cost, and it reaches $2.00 at 4,000. When a quantity rises in a straight line from 0 to 2 across 1,000 bundles, the total adds up to a triangle: \[\text{DWL} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1{,}000 \times \$2.00 = \$1{,}000 \text{ per week} .\]

Figure 7.2 shows the triangle.

A $2.00 tax per bundle. After the tax, sellers keep \(P - 2\), and so the supply curve, as the market sees it, becomes \[Q^{s} = 10(P-2) - 20 = 10P - 40 .\] Now set demand equal to this new supply curve, \[100 - 10P = 10P - 40 \;\Longrightarrow\; 140 = 20P \;\Longrightarrow\; P = 7 ,\] and \(Q = 100 - 70 = 30\). So buyers pay $7 a bundle and sellers keep $5, and the town collects \(\$2 \times 3{,}000 = \$6{,}000\) a week.

The bike-share market before the spillover is counted. \[80 - 10P = 10P - 20 \;\Longrightarrow\; 100 = 20P \;\Longrightarrow\; P^{*} = 5 ,\] and \(Q^{*} = 80 - 10(5) = 30\), or 3,000 rides a day. If we substitute into supply instead, we get \(10(5) - 20 = 30\), so the two routes agree.

The rides that do not happen.

\(Q\) (00s) Social value Cost Society’s gain
30 \(\frac{100-30}{10} = 7.00\) \(\frac{30+20}{10} = 5.00\) \(+2.00\)
35 \(\frac{100-35}{10} = 6.50\) \(\frac{35+20}{10} = 5.50\) \(+1.00\)
40 \(\frac{100-40}{10} = 6.00\) \(\frac{40+20}{10} = 6.00\) \(\phantom{+}0.00\)
45 \(\frac{100-45}{10} = 5.50\) \(\frac{45+20}{10} = 6.50\) \(-1.00\)

At 3,000 rides, where the market stops, one more ride is worth $7.00 to society and costs $5.00 to provide. At 3,500 rides the gain is still $1.00, and by 4,000 it has fallen to zero. So the optimum solves \[\frac{100-Q}{10} = \frac{Q+20}{10} \;\Longrightarrow\; 100 - Q = Q + 20 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2Q = 80 \;\Longrightarrow\; Q = 40 ,\] at a height of $6.00. So there are a thousand rides a day that would be worth more than they cost, and they never happen.

Two factories on one river. Northside Mill and Harbor Chemical each discharge 80 tons a month into the same river, for a total of 160 tons. The town wants to bring that total down to 100 tons, which means cutting 60 tons between them. Cutting one ton costs the Mill $50, and it costs Harbor $150.

Suppose the rule requires each firm to cut 30 tons: \[\text{Mill: } 30 \times \$50 = \$1{,}500 , \qquad \text{Harbor: } 30 \times \$150 = \$4{,}500 ,\] for a total of $6,000 a month, and the target is met exactly.

The same cap through permits. The town issues 100 permits, 50 to each firm. Because there are only two firms, the price has to be negotiated between them, and any price between the Mill’s $50 and Harbor’s $150 suits both. They agree on $100.

The Mill can cut a ton for $50, and each permit it holds is worth $100. So if it cuts a ton and sells the permit that this frees up, the Mill nets $50. It cuts 60 tons, which is as many as Harbor will buy permits for. That costs \(60 \times \$50 = \$3{,}000\). The Mill is then emitting 20 tons and holding 30 spare permits, which it sells for \(30 \times \$100 = \$3{,}000\). So its net cost is $0.

Harbor cuts nothing, because cutting costs it $150 a ton while a permit costs $100. So it buys the Mill’s 30 permits for $3,000 and goes on emitting 80 tons.

Emissions total \(20 + 80 = 100\) tons, the same cap that the rule achieved. The only real resources used up are the Mill’s $3,000 of abatement. The $3,000 in permit payments is a transfer between the two firms. It buys no cleanup and destroys nothing. So the true cost is $3,000, compared with $6,000 under the rule.

Figure 7.3 sets the two bills side by side.

Two plants and a 40-ton target. Plant A can cut a ton for $80, and plant B for $30. Each plant emits 50 tons, and the state wants the total brought down to 60, so 40 tons must be cut.

Under a uniform rule, each plant cuts 20 tons, and that costs \(20 \times \$80 + 20 \times \$30 = \$1{,}600 + \$600 = \$2{,}200\).

Now suppose the state issues 60 permits instead, 30 to each plant, and the two plants agree on a price of $55. Plant B cuts all 40 tons, which costs \(40 \times \$30 = \$1{,}200\), and emits 10. It sells 20 permits for \(20 \times \$55 = \$1{,}100\), so its net cost is $100. Plant A cuts nothing and buys those 20 permits for $1,100. Emissions are \(10 + 50 = 60\), and society’s real abatement bill is B’s $1,200.

For both firms to prefer trading to the rule, the permit price must lie between the two firms’ own costs. Take plant A first. It buys 20 permits, instead of cutting 20 tons at a cost of $1,600, when \(20p < 1{,}600\), that is, when \(p < \$80\). Now take plant B. It cuts 40 and sells 20, at a net cost of \(1{,}200 - 20p\). It prefers that to the $600 it would pay under the rule when \(1{,}200 - 20p < 600\), that is, when \(p > \$30\). Both conditions hold when \[\$30 < p < \$80 .\] At a price of $80, plant A is indifferent between buying a permit and cutting the ton itself. At $30, it is plant B that is indifferent. So each end of the range is one firm’s own cost per ton.

A café, a bookshop and a Friday night. Live music on Fridays at Café Solana is worth $400 a week to the café. The noise carries into Harriman Books next door, and it costs the bookshop $600 a week in lost evening sales. Under the law, the café has the right to play.

Quiet is worth $600 and the music is worth $400. So quiet creates more value, and yet the law allows the outcome that creates less.

Suppose the bookshop offers the café $500 to keep Fridays quiet. The café compares that $500 with the $400 value it places on the music, and it accepts. The bookshop avoids $600 of losses and pays $500, so it is $100 better off. The café gives up $400 of value and receives $500, so it too is $100 better off. No regulator is involved, and yet the efficient outcome is reached, because the bystander stopped being a bystander by making an offer.
