14  Monopolistic Competition

14.1 Definitions

Monopolistic competition. A market in which there are many sellers, the products are differentiated, and firms are free to enter and exit.

Consider a coffee roaster in a city of two hundred roasters, each with its own blend, its own shop and its own customers. Its output \(q\) is measured in thousands of pounds a year, its price \(P\) in dollars a pound, and its total cost is \(TC = 144 + 10q + q^{2}\) in thousands of dollars a year.

What makes the demand curve slope down. The firm’s demand curve slopes down because its product is distinguishable from the others, not because it is the only seller. If a firm raises its price above every rival’s, it loses some of its customers rather than all of them. The ones who stay are those who preferred this seller by more than the amount of the price rise.

The roaster faces its own demand curve \(P = A - 3q\), so to sell one thousand pounds more it has to cut its price by $3. A price taker would need no price cut at all.

Four steps, in this order. First, set marginal revenue equal to marginal cost and solve for the quantity. Second, take that quantity up to the demand curve to find the price. Third, take the same quantity up to average total cost to find the cost per unit. Finally, profit is price minus average total cost, multiplied by the quantity.

With demand at \(P = 74 - 3q\), so that \(MR = 74 - 6q\), and \(MC = 10 + 2q\), the roaster produces \(q = 8\) at a price of 50, its average total cost is 36, and its profit is \((50 - 36) \times 8 = 112\). With demand at \(P = 42 - 3q\) the same steps give \(q = 4\), a price of 30 and average total cost of 50, so it loses \((30 - 50) \times 4 = -80\).

Entry and exit move the firm’s own demand curve. Fewer customers at every price means the demand curve has shifted left. Read the other way, the firm must now charge less to sell any given quantity. Losses run the same mechanism backwards. When a roaster closes, its customers still want coffee, and they spread themselves among the shops that remain. So every surviving firm has more customers at any given price, and that pushes the intercept back up. The cost function does not move, whether firms are entering or leaving.

Entry moves the roaster’s intercept from 74 down to 58, a fall of $16 at every quantity. At six units it could charge \(74 - 18 = 56\) before entry and only \(58 - 18 = 40\) after it.

Excess capacity. The gap between the output a firm produces and its efficient scale.

In the long run the roaster produces 6 thousand pounds a year, and its efficient scale is 12, so its excess capacity is \(12 - 6 = 6\). Its average total cost is 40 where it could be 34.

The markup. Price minus marginal cost.

At its long-run position the roaster charges 40 and its last unit costs 22, so the markup is \(40 - 22 = 18\) dollars a pound. A price-taking firm with the same costs charges 34 for a last unit that costs 34, a markup of nothing.

Against perfect competition and monopoly. Monopolistic competition shares free entry with perfect competition, along with its consequence, zero economic profit in the long run. It shares a downward-sloping demand curve with monopoly, along with its consequences: marginal revenue below price, and therefore a price above marginal cost.

With the roaster’s costs, perfect competition gives a quantity of 12 at a price of 34, and monopolistic competition gives 6 at a price of 40. Profit is zero in both, which is why the differences between them are not about profit.

Advertising as a signal. The content of an advertisement may say nothing useful, and yet the willingness to spend on it can still be informative. Advertising separates good firms from poor ones when the customers it wins come back. A firm that expects repeat purchases can recoup a cost that a firm selling something poor, which makes just one sale, cannot.

A firm whose customer is worth $60 over all the repeat purchases will pay $20 to win one. A firm whose customer is worth $6 will not, so the campaign itself tells the buyer which kind of firm is selling.

The product-variety externality. When a new firm enters with a product of its own, the consumers who prefer that product gain surplus that the entrant does not collect. The entrant charges one price, so buyers who would have paid far more for this particular blend keep the difference.

A customer who values a new roaster’s blend at $70 pays 40 like everyone else and keeps 30 of surplus. None of that 30 is the entrant’s revenue, so it plays no part in the decision to open.

The business-stealing externality. When a new firm enters, it takes customers from firms already in the market, and their loss plays no part in its calculation.

A new roaster spends the fixed 144 to open, and some of its sales are the same customers buying from a different shop. That part of its profit is not new output but a loss to the roasters it has never met.

14.2 Worked examples

In the table, only \(q\) is given. The price is read off the demand curve, total revenue is price times quantity, and the last row is the difference between one total revenue and the next (Figure 14.1).

Solving the two conditions together. Start from \(MR = MC\) and leave \(A\) unknown: \[A - 6q = 10 + 2q \;\Longrightarrow\; A - 10 = 8q \;\Longrightarrow\; A = 8q + 10 .\] Every position in this chapter satisfies this equation: \(q = 8\) gives \(A = 74\), \(q = 6\) gives \(A = 58\), and \(q = 4\) gives \(A = 42\).

Next, from \(P = ATC\):

\[ A - 3q = \frac{144}{q} + 10 + q \;\Longrightarrow\; A = \frac{144}{q} + 10 + 4q . \tag{14.1}\]

At \(q = 4\) this requires \(A = 36 + 10 + 16 = 62\), but the loss-making firm had \(A = 42\). The shortfall of 20 is exactly the loss per unit that we found there, \(ATC(4) - P = 50 - 30 = 20\). So Equation (14.1) does more than agree with that arithmetic. It reproduces it.

Now set the expression for \(A\) from \(MR = MC\) equal to Equation (14.1). The 10 cancels from both sides, and \(4q\) comes off both sides too, leaving

\[ 4q = \frac{144}{q} \;\Longrightarrow\; 4q^{2} = 144 \;\Longrightarrow\; q^{2} = 36 \;\Longrightarrow\; q = 6 , \tag{14.2}\]

taking the positive root, because a negative quantity means nothing. Substituting back, the first equation gives \(A = 8(6) + 10 = 58\), and Equation (14.1) gives \(A = 24 + 10 + 24 = 58\). The two equations were independent of each other, so the fact that they agree is a check on the answer.

The same result, from the profit column. The table uses \(P = 58 - 3q\) and \(TC = 144 + 10q + q^{2}\):

profit \(-36\) \(-16\) \(-4\) 0 \(-4\) \(-16\) \(-36\)

The best the firm can do is to make exactly nothing, and it does that at 6. The profit row is symmetric about 6, and it is never positive, because \[(58 - 3q)q - (144 + 10q + q^{2}) = -4q^{2} + 48q - 144 = -4(q - 6)^{2} ,\] which is zero at \(q = 6\) and negative everywhere else. A profit function whose maximum is exactly zero, reached at one quantity only, is tangency written in numbers rather than drawn as geometry (Figure 14.2).

The trade that does not happen. In the long-run position, the firm sells 6 at a price of 40, and its marginal cost there is 22.

what a buyer would pay for a seventh unit \(58 - 3(7) = 37\)
what a seventh unit costs to make \(TC(7) - TC(6) = 263 - 240 = 23\)
gain if it were made and sold \(37 - 23 = 14\)

The seventh thousand pounds is worth 38.5 to its buyers in total, since their valuations run from 40 down to 37. It costs 23 to make. So making it would leave the town 15.5 better off, and yet it is not made. The reason is that the firm does not collect that value. It collects marginal revenue, which is the 37 the new unit sells for, less the $3 it must give up on each of the six existing buyers, or \(37 - 18 = 19\), against a cost of 23. That is a loss of 4, and the profit column above shows exactly \(-4\) at \(q = 7\).
