17  Modern Data Analysis in Economics

17.1 Definitions

Correlation. Two variables that move together. Here the relationship is negative, because more of one variable goes with less of the other.

Eight students record their weekly hours of tutoring and their exam scores, in invented numbers. The two with no tutoring average 84.5 and the six who were tutored average 70.5, so more tutoring goes with lower scores.

Reverse causality. \(X\) and \(Y\) move together in the data, but the influence runs from \(Y\) to \(X\), rather than from \(X\) to \(Y\).

Cities with more police officers per resident can have more crime, because a crime wave leads the city council to put more officers on the street. In the tutoring data, students who expect to do badly seek out tutoring, so the low scores came first.

Omitted variable. A third variable \(Z\), which has been left out of the comparison, and which drives both \(X\) and \(Y\).

Among schoolchildren, shoe size and reading ability are strongly correlated, but big feet do not help anyone to read. Age drives both.

Selection. The units that receive a treatment differ from the units that do not, and they differ before the treatment has done anything.

The students who sign up for tutoring are the ones already struggling. So the tutored group was headed for lower scores before the first session took place.

Counterfactual. What would have happened to the same person, firm or town had the treatment not occurred.

Take a tutored student who scored 64. The counterfactual is the score that the same student, in the same week, would have obtained without tutoring.

The fundamental problem of causal inference. The counterfactual is unobservable by definition, because a unit cannot both receive a treatment and not receive it.

A student either took the tutoring or did not, so we observe each one in a single column. The cell the comparison needs, that student’s score in the other column, is always the one that cannot be filled.

Control group. Units that do not receive the treatment. Their outcome stands in for what the treated group’s outcome would have been.

In January 2025, Fairview starts paying a $500 grant to any landlord who re-lets an empty storefront, and Grantham, one county over, does not. Grantham is then the control group, and its change in vacancies stands in for what Fairview’s change would have been without the grant.

Randomized controlled trial. Treatment is assigned by pure chance, so that a coin decides which units are treated and which units are the controls.

The coin takes no account of motivation, health or talent, so with enough subjects each of them splits roughly evenly between the groups. Economists rarely have the option, since a trial of the minimum wage would mean flipping a coin to decide which states raise it.

Experimental and observational data. Experimental data comes from a researcher who controlled how the treatment was assigned. Observational data comes from the world going about its business, and it is recorded in surveys, tax records, censuses and payrolls.

A count of the vacant storefronts on each Main Street is observational, because nobody assigned anything. Nearly all economic data is of this kind.

Panel data. Observations on many units at many dates. Each unit can then be compared both with other units and with itself at other dates.

Fairview and Grantham, with their vacant storefronts counted every December, make a small panel of two units at three dates. Fairview’s counts are 27, 24 and 18 for 2023, 2024 and 2025, and Grantham’s are 33, 30 and 27.

Natural experiment. An event, such as a law, a border or a storm, that gives a treatment to one group and not to another, in a way that is unrelated to how the groups were already doing.

On 1 April 1992, New Jersey raised its minimum wage from $4.25 to $5.05 an hour, and Pennsylvania, next door, kept its minimum at $4.25. David Card and Alan Krueger surveyed 410 fast-food restaurants on both sides of the line, before the increase and after it.

Difference-in-differences. The change in the treated group minus the change in the control group. It is an estimate of the treatment’s effect, and it is built from four averages.

Applied to Fairview and Grantham, the first subtraction, along time, removes the permanent difference between the two towns. The second, across the towns, removes the year they share, and what survives both is the estimate of the grant’s effect.

Parallel trends. Without the treatment, the treated group’s outcome would have changed by the same amount as the control group’s outcome did.

The levels need not match, because they cancel in the first subtraction. In the year before the grant, Fairview and Grantham each fell by 3, so the assumption held in the one period in which it could be checked.

Controlling for an outcome. A control variable that is itself partly produced by the process being studied. If the effect runs through that variable, holding it fixed removes that part of the effect we are trying to measure.

Blau and Kahn (2017) find that the gender wage gap among full-time workers falls from 20.7 to 8.4 points once education, experience, demographics, industry, occupation and union membership are controlled for. But if hiring or promotion steers women away from high-paying occupations, part of the gap runs through occupation, and holding occupation fixed deletes that part.

Event study. Difference-in-differences stretched over many periods. It tracks the treated and comparison outcomes for years around an event, rather than for one period before and one period after.

Kleven, Landais and Søgaard (2019) line up Danish mothers and fathers by the number of years relative to the first birth. Mothers’ earnings drop by about 30 percent around the birth and settle about 20 percent below their pre-birth path, while fathers’ earnings are essentially unchanged.

17.2 Worked examples

Tutoring hours and exam scores.

student A B C D E F G H
tutoring, hours a week 0 0 1 1 2 3 4 5
exam score 88 81 78 84 72 69 62 58

If we read along the rows, scores tend to fall as the hours of tutoring rise. The two students with no tutoring scored 88 and 81. The student with five hours scored 58. The pattern is consistent, and it is not produced by one unusual student.

Four numbers.

vacant storefronts Dec 2024, before Dec 2025, after
Fairview, grant 24 18
Grantham, no grant 30 27

We have two groups at two dates, and this table holds everything the method needs.

The first subtraction runs along time, and gives each town’s change: \[\text{Fairview: } 18 - 24 = -6 , \qquad \text{Grantham: } 27 - 30 = -3 .\] The second subtraction runs across the towns and takes the difference between those changes, \((-6) - (-3) = -3\). Figure 17.2 draws the estimate.

Two other towns, Hazel with the grant and Kirby without it, have the same two-by-two table as Fairview and Grantham, and so the same estimate of \(-3\).

The same table, a different history.

Dec 2023 Dec 2024 pre-trend Dec 2025
Hazel, grant 30 24 \(24 - 30 = -6\) 18
Kirby, none 33 30 \(30 - 33 = -3\) 27

Before any policy existed, Hazel was already improving twice as fast as Kirby. So the two paths were diverging rather than running in parallel, and the assumption the estimate rests on was false before anyone announced the grant.

Figure 17.3 sets the two histories side by side.

Full-time-equivalent employment per restaurant.

before after change
New Jersey, raise 20.44 21.03 \(21.03 - 20.44 = +0.59\)
Pennsylvania, none 23.33 21.17 \(21.17 - 23.33 = -2.16\)

So the difference in differences comes to \[0.59 - (-2.16) = 0.59 + 2.16 = +2.75 .\] If we subtract the other way, we get the same answer. Before the increase, the gap between the states was \(20.44 - 23.33 = -2.89\). After it, the gap was \(21.03 - 21.17 = -0.14\). And \((-0.14) - (-2.89) = +2.75\).

These figures are the means published in the paper’s own table. Card and Krueger report 2.76, which they computed before rounding.
