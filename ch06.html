6  Market Efficiency

6.1 Definitions

Willingness to pay. Willingness to pay is the most that a buyer would hand over for one unit of a good. If the seller charges even one cent more than that, the buyer does not trade.

For example, Maya would hand over at most $6.00 for a cup of coffee, and Ben would hand over at most $2.00. So at a price of $4.00, Maya buys and Ben does not.

Diminishing marginal utility. Each additional unit that a buyer consumes adds less satisfaction than the unit before it. So if we hold fixed what a dollar is worth to the buyer, the dollar value that the buyer places on each successive unit falls.

Consider Maya’s morning, cup by cup. Her first cup is worth $6.00 to her, her second $4.50, her third $3.00 and her fourth $1.50, so at a price of $4.00 she buys two cups and then stops.

Consumer surplus. Consumer surplus is what a buyer would have paid minus what the buyer actually pays, \(\text{CS} = \text{WTP} - P\). It measures the buyer’s gain from the trade, in dollars.

Suppose the price of a cup is $3.50. Maya would have paid $6.00, so her consumer surplus is \(6.00 - 3.50 = \$2.50\), while Priya, who would have paid exactly $3.50, gains $0.

Producer surplus. Producer surplus is what a seller is paid minus the seller’s cost, \(\text{PS} = P - \text{cost}\). On a diagram, it is the area above the supply curve and below the price, out to the quantity traded.

For example, it costs Rosa $1.50 to supply a cup from her cart. At a price of $3.00, her producer surplus on that cup is \(3.00 - 1.50 = \$1.50\).

Total surplus. Total surplus is consumer surplus plus producer surplus. It is the whole gain that the market creates for the people who take part in it.

The worked examples below use a coffee market with demand \(Q^{d} = 70 - 10P\) and supply \(Q^{s} = 10P - 10\), which clears at a price of $4 with 30 thousand cups traded a week. In that market, consumer surplus is $45,000 and producer surplus is $45,000, so total surplus is \(45 + 45 = \$90{,}000\) per week.

Efficiency. An allocation is efficient when it maximizes total surplus. In an efficient allocation, no change in who buys, who sells or how much is traded can create more value.

In the coffee market, the efficient quantity is 30 thousand cups. At 20 thousand, the marginal cup is worth $5.00 to a buyer and costs $3.00 to make, so stopping there gives up value, and at 40 thousand it is worth $3.00 and costs $5.00, so the last cups destroy value.

Market failure. Market failure is a situation in which the equilibrium of an unregulated market does not maximize total surplus.

For example, suppose a trade affects people other than its own buyer and seller. Then the value and the cost that the market acts on are not the full value and the full cost, and the quantity it trades is not the one that maximizes total surplus.

6.2 Worked examples

Consumer surplus in the coffee market. At \(P^{*} = \$4\) the market trades \(Q^{*} = 30\) thousand cups. The demand curve starts at $7. That is the choke price, and it is also the value of the first cup. From there the curve runs down to $4, which is the value of the marginal cup at 30 thousand. So the region is a triangle. Its base is 30, and its height is \(7 - 4 = 3\): \[\text{CS} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 30 \times (7 - 4) = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 30 \times 3 = 45 .\] So buyers gain $45,000 a week in total.

Figure 6.1 shows this triangle.

A price of $5. Now suppose the price rises to $5. At that price, quantity demanded falls to 20 thousand cups, and \[\text{CS} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 20 \times (7 - 5) = 20 ,\] so consumer surplus falls from $45,000 to $20,000. That is a loss of $25,000, and the loss has two parts. First, buyers pay $1 more on each of the 20 thousand cups that they still buy, and that comes to $20,000. Second, some buyers drop out and no longer buy. They lose the surplus they were earning on 10 thousand cups, which were worth between $4 and $5 to them. That lost surplus is a small triangle, \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 1 = \$5{,}000\). Add the two parts together, and we are back at $25,000.

Figure 6.2 shows the two parts of the loss.

Producer surplus in the coffee market. At \(P^{*} = \$4\) the supply curve starts at $1. That is the minimum supply price, the cost of the cheapest cup to make. The curve then runs up to $4, which is the cost of the marginal cup at 30 thousand: \[\text{PS} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 30 \times (4 - 1) = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 30 \times 3 = 45 .\] So sellers gain $45,000 a week. As on the buyers’ side, the marginal cup earns exactly zero surplus, because at the margin the price equals the cost.

Figure 6.3 shows the sellers’ triangle.

Is 30 thousand the right quantity?

\(Q\) (000s) Marginal value Marginal cost Gap
20 \(\frac{70-20}{10} = 5.00\) \(\frac{20+10}{10} = 3.00\) \(+2.00\)
25 \(\frac{70-25}{10} = 4.50\) \(\frac{25+10}{10} = 3.50\) \(+1.00\)
30 \(\frac{70-30}{10} = 4.00\) \(\frac{30+10}{10} = 4.00\) \(\phantom{+}0.00\)
35 \(\frac{70-35}{10} = 3.50\) \(\frac{35+10}{10} = 4.50\) \(-1.00\)
40 \(\frac{70-40}{10} = 3.00\) \(\frac{40+10}{10} = 5.00\) \(-2.00\)

Below 30 thousand, the gap between value and cost is positive. Each further cup is worth more than it costs, so stopping early gives up gains that were there to be had. Above 30 thousand, the gap is negative. Each further cup costs more than it is worth, so producing it destroys value. At 30 thousand, the gap is zero. That is the quantity at which total surplus reaches its maximum. It is also the quantity at which the market settles, without anyone giving instructions.

A cap on the quantity. Suppose a licensing rule permits only 20 thousand cups per week, and the price is free to adjust. The trades between 20 and 30 thousand cups were creating value. Over that whole range, marginal value was above marginal cost, so the gap was positive. The licensing rule now prohibits those trades.

The surplus that is lost is a triangle. At 20 thousand cups, the gap between marginal value and marginal cost is $2.00. The gap then shrinks to zero at 30 thousand. So the loss is \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 2 = \$10{,}000\), and total surplus falls from $90,000 to $80,000.

There is a second, independent route to the same figure. On the \(Q\)th cup, the gain from trade is the difference between marginal value and marginal cost, \[\frac{70 - Q}{10} - \frac{Q + 10}{10} = \frac{60 - 2Q}{10} = 6 - 0.2Q ,\] which is $6.00 on the first cup and $2.00 on the twenty-thousandth. The gain falls along a straight line, so take the average over that range: \((6.00 + 2.00)/2 = \$4.00\). Then multiply the average by the number of cups, and \(4.00 \times 20 = \$80{,}000\).

The two routes give the same answer. Each of them is an exact computation of the same quantity. So if they disagreed, that would point to a slip in the arithmetic, rather than to a disagreement about the economics.
