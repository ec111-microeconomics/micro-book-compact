16  Asymmetric Information

16.1 Definitions

Symmetric information. Both parties to a trade know the same things about the object of the trade. This does not mean that everything is known. It means that whatever is known is known by both.

Suppose buyer and seller can both see that a used car is high quality, worth $8,000 to its owner and $10,000 to a buyer. Then it sells at a price such as $9,000, and each side gains $1,000.

Asymmetric information. One party to a trade knows something about the object of the trade that the other party does not.

The seller of a used car has driven it and knows its condition. The buyer has not, and a test drive and an hour with the service history do not settle the question.

Adverse selection, or hidden type. One party knows a characteristic of what is being traded, and the other party cannot observe it. The characteristic is fixed before any bargaining takes place.

A used car is either good or bad before anyone bargains over it, and no contract can alter that. So a single price must serve for cars of different worth.

Moral hazard, or hidden action. One party takes an action that the other party cannot observe. The action is chosen after the contract has been signed.

A chief executive’s effort does not exist until the executive supplies it, after signing. The board sees only whether the outcome is $400 thousand or $200 thousand.

Expected value. Let \(\lambda\) stand for the share of the lot that is high quality, so that \(1 - \lambda\) is the share that is low quality. Then the average value of a car drawn from the lot is \[\lambda\,v_B^H + (1-\lambda)\,v_B^L ,\] where \(v_B^H\) is the value of a high-quality car to a buyer, and \(v_B^L\) is what a low-quality car is worth to a buyer. This is called an expected value. Expected value is just a name for a weighted average, and nothing more.

With sixty good cars in a hundred, \(\lambda = 0.6\), and with \(v_B^H = 10\) and \(v_B^L = 4\) in thousands, the expected value is \(0.6(10) + 0.4(4) = 7.6\), or $7,600. No car on the lot is worth $7,600.

Reservation value. The least a seller will accept for a good, or the most a buyer will pay for it. Below the seller’s number, there is no sale. Above the buyer’s number, there is no purchase. A trade can happen between the two.

The owner of a high-quality car will not accept less than $8,000 for it, and a buyer will not pay more than $10,000. A price anywhere between the two leaves both better off.

Private information. Something that one party to a trade knows and the other party does not observe.

The seller knows whether the car is one of the sixty good ones or one of the forty bad ones. The buyer knows only the shares, 60 and 40 in a hundred.

The threshold at which the good side withdraws. A high-quality car reaches the market only if the most a buyer will pay reaches the least its owner will accept, \(\lambda\,v_B^H + (1-\lambda)\,v_B^L \ge v_S^H\). Solved for the share of good cars, the condition is \[\lambda \;\ge\; \lambda^{*} \equiv \frac{v_S^H - v_B^L}{v_B^H - v_B^L} .\]

With the four numbers of this market, \(\lambda^{*} = (8 - 4)/(10 - 4) = \tfrac23\). A lot with sixty good cars in a hundred falls short, so its good cars are not offered, and \(60 \times \$2{,}000 = \$120{,}000\) of surplus is lost.

Signal. A costly action taken to reveal private information. It is credible because the incentive to take it differs between the types, most often because the action costs more for the party who would take it falsely.

A warranty that pays the buyer \(W\) after a major repair is a signal. It costs a high-quality seller \(0.10W\) and a low-quality seller \(0.60W\), and any warranty between $10,000 and $20,000 separates the two.

Screening. The uninformed party offers a set of options, designed so that different types choose differently. It then learns the type from the choice, without observing the type directly.

An insurer offers a high-deductible policy at a low premium and a low-deductible policy at a high premium. An applicant who expects few claims takes the high deductible, and one who expects many claims does not.

The principal–agent problem. One party, the principal, hires another party, the agent, to act on its behalf, and the principal cannot observe what the agent does.

Shareholders and a chief executive are one example, and an employer and an employee are another. In each case the principal can verify hours and presence, but not effort.

Incentive compatibility constraint. The requirement that, given the contract, the action the agent prefers is the action the principal wants.

For the chief executive this reads \(0.75w_H + 0.25w_L - 36 \ge 0.45w_H + 0.55w_L\), which simplifies to \(w_H - w_L \ge 120\). Only the gap between the two wages matters.

Participation constraint. The requirement that the agent is at least as well off accepting the contract as taking the next-best alternative.

The executive’s next-best job is worth $24 thousand, so the contract must satisfy \(0.75w_H + 0.25w_L - 36 \ge 24\). This depends on the level of pay, not the gap.

Limited liability. A wage cannot be negative. The agent can be paid nothing, but the agent cannot be required to pay the principal.

Selling the firm to the executive for a fee of 290 would give a wage of \(200 - 290 = -90\) in the bad state. Limited liability rules that out, so the board sets \(w_L = 0\) and \(w_H = 120\).

Agency cost. The difference between what the principal pays when the action is hidden and what the principal would pay if the action could be observed.

With effort observable the board pays \(24 + 36 = 60\). With effort hidden it pays \(0.75 \times 120 = 90\), so the agency cost is \(90 - 60 = 30\), and it goes to the executive.

16.2 Worked examples

The value of the lot, and of one car from it.

how many each worth together
high quality 60 $10,000 $600,000
low quality 40 $4,000 $160,000
all 100 $760,000

Multiply each count by its value. The two products are \(60 \times 10{,}000 = 600{,}000\) and \(40 \times 4{,}000 = 160{,}000\), and adding them gives \(760{,}000\). That total is the value to buyers of every car on the lot. It is not a price, and nobody pays it.

Dividing that total by the number of cars gives the value of a single car drawn from the lot: \[\frac{\$760{,}000}{100} = \$7{,}600 .\] As a check, the answer lies between $4,000 and $10,000, and it is closer to $10,000, because most of the lot is high quality. Any answer outside that range would be wrong.

No car on the lot is worth $7,600. Every car is worth either $10,000 or $4,000. The average is a property of the whole collection, rather than of any member of it.

The four numbers in this market.

seller will not accept below buyer will not pay above gap
high quality $8,000 $10,000 $2,000
low quality $2,000 $4,000 $2,000

In symbols, these values are \[v_S^H = 8, \qquad v_B^H = 10, \qquad v_S^L = 2, \qquad v_B^L = 4 ,\] measured in thousands of dollars.

Take a low-quality car first. It is worth $2,000 to its owner and $4,000 to a buyer. Suppose it sells at a price of $3,000. The seller gives up something worth $2,000 and receives $3,000, so the seller gains $1,000. The buyer pays $3,000 for something worth $4,000, so the buyer also gains $1,000. A high-quality car works the same way. It is worth $8,000 to its owner and $10,000 to a buyer, so at a price of $9,000 each side gains $1,000.

The gap is the same size on both rows, and that is a deliberate feature of the example. If the high-quality gap were smaller, we could say that the market destroys the trades that matter least. Here the two gaps are identical. So the trades that are about to disappear are worth exactly as much as the ones that survive.

Figure 16.1 draws the buyer’s ceiling against the good owner’s floor for these four numbers, crossing at \(\lambda^{*} = \tfrac23\).

What the same promise costs each type.

repairs per hundred cars expected cost of the warranty
high quality 10 \(0.10\,W\)
low quality 60 \(0.60\,W\)

A high-quality seller expects to pay out on 10 cars in every 100, so the warranty costs that seller \(\tfrac{10}{100}W = 0.10W\). A low-quality seller expects to pay out on 60 in 100, so for that seller it costs \(\tfrac{60}{100}W = 0.60W\). The ratio of the two is \(0.60W / 0.10W = 6\). In other words, the identical promise is six times as expensive for the seller of a bad car.

Nothing about the warranty itself distinguishes the two sellers, since the words of the promise are the same. What differs is how much the promise will cost to keep. That cost is determined by the very characteristic that the buyer cannot observe. A seller cannot make a bad car cheap to warrant, and that is precisely why offering the warranty is informative.

Figure 16.2 draws what each seller nets as the warranty grows. The low type’s line falls six times as steeply, and the band runs from \(W = 10\) to \(W = 20\).

A board hiring a chief executive.

good outcome $400 bad outcome $200 average output
effort supplied 75 in 100 25 in 100 $350
effort not supplied 45 in 100 55 in 100 $290

All figures are in thousands. The two averages use the arithmetic from Section 16.1: \[\text{with effort: } 0.75(400) + 0.25(200) = 300 + 50 = 350 ,\] \[\text{without effort: } 0.45(400) + 0.55(200) = 180 + 110 = 290 .\] Supplying effort costs the executive $36 in forgone leisure and attention. The executive’s next-best job is worth $24.

What makes the problem hard is that the outcome is an imperfect guide to effort. Forty-five in every hundred executives who supply no effort still deliver the good outcome. And twenty-five in every hundred who do supply effort still deliver the bad outcome. So a good year is not proof of effort, and a bad year is not proof that effort was absent.
