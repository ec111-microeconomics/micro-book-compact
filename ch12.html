12  Monopoly

12.1 Definitions

Monopoly. A firm that is the sole seller of a product for which there are no close substitutes.

The sensor firm is the sole maker of a specialized industrial sensor, and a patent protects it. It faces market demand \(P = 100 - 5Q\), so if it chooses \(Q = 4\) the demand curve answers \(P = 100 - 20 = 80\).

How a monopoly arises. There are three kinds of barrier. A key resource held in one firm’s hands, for example control of the essential input. A government grant, such as a patent, a copyright or a license, where the law itself keeps rivals out. A natural monopoly, where one firm can serve the whole market more cheaply than two firms could. In that case the cost structure itself does the excluding.

The sensor firm’s barrier is a government grant. Its patent gives it the exclusive right to sell, typically for 20 years.

Barriers to entry. In a competitive market, profit attracted new entrants, and the entrants competed it away. A monopoly persists only because something blocks the door. If there is no barrier, there is no monopoly. Free entry always wears market power down.

The sensor firm earns $120 thousand a month. In a competitive market a profit of that size would draw entrants, but here the patent holds the door shut.

Natural monopoly. An industry in which a single firm can supply the whole market at a lower average cost than two or more firms could.

A city’s electricity grid costs \(TC = 160 + 10Q\), so \(ATC = 160/Q + 10\). Suppose 8,000 homes are to be served. One firm serving all of them has an average cost of $30 a home, while two firms serving 4,000 each have $50, because each pays the $160 thousand fixed cost for its own poles and wires.

Why marginal revenue falls short of the price. To sell more, the firm must let the price fall. The lower price applies to every unit, including those it was already selling, rather than only to the new one. So \(MR < P\) for any firm that faces a downward-sloping demand curve, and the gap between them is the price effect. A competitive firm escapes the price effect because its demand curve is flat, so \(MR = P\).

To sell 8 thousand sensors rather than 7 thousand, the firm cuts the price from $65 to $60. The output effect is \(+60\) and the price effect is \(7 \times (-5) = -35\), so marginal revenue is only 25.

The profit rectangle, and how to build it. Start at the point where marginal revenue meets marginal cost, which fixes \(Q_{m}\). Climb straight up to the demand curve to get \(P_{m}\). Then drop down to the \(ATC\) curve at the same quantity. The gap is profit per unit. The profit rectangle runs from zero to \(Q_{m}\), with its top at the price and its bottom at \(ATC\).

For the sensor firm, with \(TC = 200 + 20Q\), \(\pi = (60 - 45) \times 8 = 120\) (Figure 12.1). If the license fee doubled the fixed cost to 400, \(ATC\) at 8 would be 70, the rectangle would sit above the price line, and \(\pi = (60 - 70) \times 8 = -80\).

Monopoly compared with perfect competition. The efficient quantity is found by making every unit whose value covers its cost, and stopping where value falls to cost. Competition pushes the price down to marginal cost. The monopolist is a case of trading too little. The deadweight loss triangle sits between the marginal cost line and the demand curve, and spans the quantities from \(Q_{m}\) to \(Q^{*}\). The profit is a transfer and the triangle is the loss. A monopoly price works like a private tax. It drives a wedge between what buyers pay and what production costs. The difference is that the revenue goes to the firm, rather than to a treasury.

A competitive sensor market would sell 16 thousand at $20, and the monopolist sells 8 thousand at $60. Of the $640 thousand of surplus, $320 thousand is transferred to the firm and $160 thousand is destroyed (Figure 12.2).

Price discrimination. Selling the same good to different buyers at different prices, when it costs the same to serve each buyer.

At its single price of $60, the sensor firm lets the buyers of the second thousand, whose sensors were worth $90 to them, keep $30 each. It also excludes units 9 through 16, worth from $55 down to $20, that would be profitable to serve at a lower price.

Regulating a natural monopoly. For a natural monopoly of this kind, average total cost is above marginal cost everywhere. So a price equal to marginal cost is necessarily below average cost, and a price that covers average cost is necessarily above marginal cost. With a single price per unit, only a subsidy avoids the choice.

If the city distributor, facing \(P = 70 - 5Q\), is capped at its marginal cost of $10, it connects all 12 thousand homes worth connecting but loses its whole fixed cost of $160 thousand a month. Capping it at average cost lets the firm break even at $30 and leaves homes 8,000 to 12,000 unserved (Figure 12.3).

12.2 Worked examples

The sensor firm’s revenue schedule.

1 95 \(95 \times 1 = 95\) \(95 - 0 = 95\)
2 90 \(90 \times 2 = 180\) \(180 - 95 = 85\)
3 85 \(85 \times 3 = 255\) \(255 - 180 = 75\)
4 80 \(80 \times 4 = 320\) \(320 - 255 = 65\)

7 65 \(65 \times 7 = 455\) \(455 - 420 = 35\)

In the table, only \(Q\) is given. The price is read off the demand curve. Total revenue is price times quantity. Marginal revenue is the difference between successive total revenues. So the entry against each \(Q\) is the revenue added by the \(Q\)th thousand sensors, and strictly it belongs between \(Q-1\) and \(Q\).

The margins shrink by $10 a row, following the marginal revenue column, and the rows left out between 2 and 7 follow the same pattern. The firm produces \(Q_{m} = 8\) thousand sensors, where marginal revenue meets marginal cost (Figure 12.1).

The units the monopolist refuses to make.

unit (000s) worth to its buyer marginal cost what happens
9 \(100 - 45 = 55\) 20 worth making, never made
12 \(100 - 60 = 40\) 20 worth making, never made
16 \(100 - 80 = 20\) 20 the last one worth making, never made

Every unit from the ninth thousand to the sixteenth is valued at or above its cost, and none of them is traded. Take the ninth thousand. Its buyers value it at $55 a sensor, and it costs $20 a sensor to make. So that trade would create $35 a sensor, or $35 thousand in all, and it does not happen (Figure 12.2).

Perfect price discrimination. Suppose the firm knew every buyer’s exact valuation and charged each buyer precisely that amount. Then every unit worth at least $20 is sold. Each buyer pays their own valuation, and any valuation above $20 leaves the firm a margin. So output reaches \(Q = 16\), which is the efficient quantity. Revenue is the whole area under the demand curve out to 16, \[\tfrac{1}{2}(100 + 20) \times 16 = 960 ,\] and profit is \(960 - (200 + 320) = 440\), compared with 120 under a single price.

consumer surplus deadweight loss total surplus
one price 160 160 480
perfect discrimination 0 0 640

Consumer surplus is zero. Every buyer pays exactly what the unit was worth to them, so nobody keeps anything. Deadweight loss is zero as well, and total surplus is the full 640, which is the same as under perfect competition.

Capping the price at average cost. Now let the firm break even, and let the equation find the point: \[70 - 5Q = \frac{160}{Q} + 10 .\] Multiplying through by \(Q\) and collecting terms gives \(5Q^{2} - 60Q + 160 = 0\), or \(Q^{2} - 12Q + 32 = 0\), which factors as \((Q - 4)(Q - 8) = 0\).

There are two break-even points, at \(Q = 4\) and \(Q = 8\). The larger one serves more homes at a lower price, so we take \(Q = 8\) and \(P = 70 - 40 = 30\). To check, \(ATC = 160/8 + 10 = 30 = P\), so the firm survives with no subsidy.

The price of $30 still exceeds the marginal cost of $10, so homes 8,000 to 12,000 remain unserved (Figure 12.3) and \[DWL = \tfrac{1}{2}(30 - 10)(12 - 8) = 40 .\]
