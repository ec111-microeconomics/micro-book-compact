5  Price Controls and Taxes

5.1 Definitions

Price ceiling. A legal maximum on the price at which a good can be sold. The standard example is rent control, and there the motive is usually to protect buyers.

Take the coffee market, where \(Q^{d} = 70 - 10P\) and \(Q^{s} = 10P - 10\), which clears at a price of $4 with 30 thousand cups sold a week. A ceiling of $5 sits above that price and forbids nothing that was going to happen anyway, whereas a ceiling of $3 sits below it, and so $3 becomes the price that holds.

Price floor. A legal minimum on the price at which a good can be sold. The standard example here is the minimum wage, and the motive is usually to protect sellers.

In the same coffee market, a floor of $5 sits above the $4 equilibrium, so it forbids the price the market was heading toward and it binds. A minimum wage works in the same way: where the labor market would clear at $8 an hour, a floor of $10 is binding.

5.2 Worked examples

A binding ceiling at $3. In this case the ceiling binds, so the price is $3. The demand and supply schedules both stay as they were. The only thing that has been fixed is the price. \[Q^{d} = 70 - 10(3) = 40 , \qquad Q^{s} = 10(3) - 10 = 20 .\] So the shortage is 20 thousand cups per week. The quantity actually traded is also 20 thousand, because a cup that was never produced cannot be bought by anybody.

Ordinarily, a shortage like this would push the price back up to $4, and the shortage would close itself. But the ceiling forbids that price. So the mechanism that would have closed the gap no longer operates, and the shortage persists for as long as the ceiling is in place.

Figure 5.1 shows this market.

A binding floor at $5. \[Q^{d} = 70 - 10(5) = 20 , \qquad Q^{s} = 10(5) - 10 = 40 .\] The surplus is 20 thousand cups that sellers would supply but buyers will not take, whereas the ceiling produced unmet demand. The price cannot fall back to $4 and clear it away, so the surplus persists.

The algebra gives the same answer. Setting demand equal to supply, \(150 - 10W = 10W - 10\), gives \(160 = 20W\), so \(W^{*} = \$8\) and \(L^{*} = 70\).

At $8, every teenager who is willing to work at $8 has a job, and every firm that is willing to pay $8 has the workers it wants. So before the floor is introduced, there is no involuntary unemployment in this market.

A floor at $10. Ten is above eight, so this floor is binding. \[L^{d} = 150 - 10(10) = 50 , \qquad L^{s} = 10(10) - 10 = 90 .\] The surplus is 40 thousand workers. A surplus of labor is what we call unemployment.

That 40 thousand is really made up of two things, which are equal here by construction. Employment fell from 70 to 50, so 20 thousand jobs were destroyed. At the same time, labor supply rose from 70 to 90, so 20 thousand more were drawn into the search. They came because $10 an hour is worth working for, whereas $8 was not. That second group were not unemployed before the floor, because they were not looking for work.

Figure 5.3 shows this market.

Sellers decide how much to produce on the basis of what they keep. With a $1 tax, a till price of \(P\) leaves them with \(P - 1\). So wherever the supply equation used to have \(P\), it now has \(P-1\): \[Q^{s} = 10(P-1) - 10 = 10P - 20 .\]

Solving, and checking both ways. \[70 - 10P = 10P - 20 \;\Longrightarrow\; 90 = 20P \;\Longrightarrow\; P = \$4.50 .\] Buyers pay the till price, \(P_{B} = \$4.50\). Sellers keep \(P_{S} = 4.50 - 1.00 = \$3.50\), which is the till price less the tax. The quantity traded is 25 thousand cups.

There are two ways to check it. Demand at the buyers’ price gives \(70 - 10(4.50) = 25\). The original supply curve at the sellers’ price gives \(10(3.50) - 10 = 25\). So the buyers’ price goes into the demand equation, and the sellers’ price goes into the original supply equation.

The burden, and why it is even.

Buyers: \(4.50 - 4.00\) \(+\$0.50\)
Sellers: \(3.50 - 4.00\) \(-\$0.50\)
Check: \(0.50 + 0.50\) \(= \$1.00\)

The burden splits evenly because, at this equilibrium, the elasticities of demand and supply are equal: \[|E_{d}| = 10 \times \tfrac{4}{30} = 1.33 , \qquad E_{s} = 10 \times \tfrac{4}{30} = 1.33 .\] The equal split is a choice we made in designing the example. The general rule is that the more inelastic side bears more of the tax. This market has deliberately been made symmetric, so that attention falls on the machinery rather than on how the burden is split.

Now change exactly one thing. The same $1 tax is collected from the buyer at the register instead. Buyers then respond to their all-in cost, \(P + 1\): \[Q^{d} = 70 - 10(P+1) = 60 - 10P .\] The intercept of the demand curve falls from 70 to 60, so the whole curve shifts down by exactly the tax. Supply returns to its original form, \(Q^{s} = 10P - 10\), because sellers now remit nothing to the government.

Solving the buyer-side tax. \[60 - 10P = 10P - 10 \;\Longrightarrow\; 70 = 20P \;\Longrightarrow\; P = \$3.50 .\] The till price is now $3.50, and that is what sellers keep. Buyers pay \(3.50 + 1.00 = \$4.50\) all in. The quantity is 25 thousand cups.

Take cup 26 thousand as an example. A buyer values it at $4.40, and a seller can make it for $3.60. So the trade would create 80 cents of value, and both sides want it to happen. But for the trade to happen, the seller must receive at least $3.60. With the $1 tax on top, that means the buyer must pay at least $4.60. The buyer, however, will pay at most $4.40. No price satisfies both of them. Eighty cents of gain cannot cover a dollar wedge.

The same is true of every trade whose gain is smaller than the tax. It fails to happen, even though both sides still wanted it.
