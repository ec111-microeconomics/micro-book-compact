15  Consumer Choice

15.1 Definitions

Bundle. A list of quantities, so much of one good and so much of another. When there are two goods, it is just a pair of numbers, \((x, y)\).

The buyer in this chapter has $120 a week to spend and faces \(p_y = \$2\) and \(p_x = \$3\). The bundle \((20,30)\) means 20 units of \(x\) and 30 units of \(y\).

The three assumptions. More is better: a bundle that has more of both goods is preferred. Completeness: any two bundles can be compared with each other. Consistency: if \(A\) is preferred to \(B\) and \(B\) is preferred to \(C\), then \(A\) is preferred to \(C\).

By more is better, the buyer prefers \((40,60)\) to \((20,30)\), because it has more of both goods. Nothing in the list says the buyer is selfish, well informed or sensible.

Utility. A number attached to a bundle, arranged so that a preferred bundle gets a higher number. Only the ordering of those numbers carries meaning.

With \(U(x,y) = \sqrt{xy}\), the bundle \((20,30)\) scores \(\sqrt{600} \approx 24.5\) and \((10,30)\) scores \(\sqrt{300} \approx 17.3\). All that tells us is that the buyer prefers the first. It does not say the first is 1.4 times as good.

Indifference curve. The set of bundles among which the buyer is indifferent, with every point on it delivering the same utility. For this function it is the graph of \(y = k/x\), which is a rectangular hyperbola. To find the curve through a particular bundle, multiply its two coordinates together.

Through \((20,30)\) the constant is \(k = 20 \times 30 = 600\). So \((10,60)\) and \((60,10)\) lie on the same curve, \(y = 600/x\), and the buyer is indifferent among all three bundles.

Marginal rate of substitution. The amount of \(y\) that the buyer will give up for one more unit of \(x\), with utility held constant. It is the slope of the indifference curve without its minus sign, and we write it as \(MRS\).

Moving along \(y = 600/x\) from \((10,60)\) to \((20,30)\), the buyer gives up 30 units of \(y\) for 10 of \(x\), an average of 3 per unit. From \((20,30)\) to \((40,15)\) the rate is only \(15/20 = 0.75\), so the rate falls as \(x\) rises.

The budget constraint. With two goods, prices \(p_x\) and \(p_y\) and income \(m\), the bundles that exhaust income are the ones that satisfy \(p_x x + p_y y = m\). Every coefficient is a price, and the right-hand side is income. So nothing in the equation refers to taste.

Here the constraint is \(3x + 2y = 120\), or \(y = 60 - 1.5x\). The bundle \((16,36)\) exhausts the income exactly, since \(48 + 72 = 120\).

The slope is a rate of exchange between goods. The size of the slope, 1.5, is not an amount of money. Three dollars buys one unit of \(x\), and three dollars also buys one and a half units of \(y\). So the price of \(x\), measured in units of \(y\), is 1.5. To get one more unit of \(x\), the market requires the buyer to give up 1.5 units of \(y\).

If \(x\) cost $6 instead, the ratio would be \(6/2 = 3\), and each unit of \(x\) would cost 3 units of \(y\). The line would be steeper.

The tangency condition. The only bundle from which the buyer wants no move is the one where the buyer’s rate and the market’s rate coincide: \[MRS = \frac{p_x}{p_y} , \qquad\text{which here reads}\qquad \frac{y}{x} = \frac{p_x}{p_y} .\] For a line to be tangent to a curve means that they have equal slopes at a shared point. So the optimum is a tangency.

At \((10,45)\) the buyer’s rate is \(45/10 = 4.5\) against the market’s 1.5, so at the margin \(x\) is worth more than it costs and the buyer buys more of it. At \((20,30)\) the condition reads \(30/20 = 1.5\) against \(3/2 = 1.5\), so it holds exactly.

Marginal utility. The extra utility from one more unit of a good, holding the other good fixed. Mathematically, it is a partial derivative. For \(U = x^{1/2}y^{1/2}\), \[MU_x = \frac{\partial U}{\partial x} = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{y}{x}} , \qquad MU_y = \frac{\partial U}{\partial y} = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{x}{y}} .\]

With \(y = 30\), the marginal utility of \(x\) falls from 0.866 at \(x = 10\) to 0.612 at \(x = 20\), and to 0.433 at \(x = 40\).

Equal marginal utility per dollar. At the optimum \[\frac{MU_x}{p_x} = \mu = \frac{MU_y}{p_y} ,\] so the last dollar spent on each good buys the same amount of utility.

At \((20,30)\), \(MU_x/p_x = 0.6124/3 = 0.2041\) and \(MU_y/p_y = 0.4082/2 = 0.2041\). Dividing one condition by the other gives \(y/x = p_x/p_y\), and putting that into the budget gives the chosen bundle,

\[ x^{*} = \frac{m}{2p_x} , \qquad y^{*} = \frac{m}{2p_y} . \tag{15.1}\]

A demand function. A rule that maps each price to the quantity a buyer chooses when doing the best that is available at that price. In effect it is a solved maximization problem, with one answer for each price.

With \(m = 120\), Equation (15.1) gives \(x^{*} = 60/p_x\). Spending on \(x\) is then \(p_x x^{*} = \$60\) whatever the price, so half the budget always goes on each good.

Slutsky compensation. Enough income is given to the buyer for the original bundle to be affordable at the new prices. Costing \(A = (20,30)\) at the new prices gives \[m' = p_x^{\text{new}}x_A + p_y y_A = 6(20) + 2(30) = 120 + 60 = \$180 ,\] which is $60 more than the buyer had before.

With $180 and \(p_x = \$6\), the buyer chooses \(C = (15,45)\), since \(180/12 = 15\) and \(180/4 = 45\).

Income and substitution effects. When the price of \(x\) rises, the good becomes dearer relative to \(y\), and the buyer also becomes poorer. The substitution effect is the move from \(A\) to \(C\), which changes the relative price while purchasing power is held fixed. The income effect is the move from \(C\) to \(B\), which takes the compensation away, with prices unchanged.

When \(p_x\) doubles from $3 to $6, the buyer moves from \(A = (20,30)\) to \(B = (10,30)\). The substitution effect takes \(x\) from 20 to 15 and the income effect from 15 to 10, so each is \(-5\) and together they make \(-10\).

Revealed preference. If a bundle is chosen when another bundle was affordable, the chosen bundle is at least as good as the other. No utility function need exist for the principle to apply. It concerns choices and prices alone.

Under the compensated income of $180, bundle \(A\) costs \(6(20) + 2(30) = 180\), so it was affordable, and yet the buyer chose \(C\). So \(C\) is at least as good as \(A\).

Giffen good. A good whose ordinary demand curve slopes upward. This happens when the income effect is positive and large enough to outweigh the substitution effect.

Such goods are rare. The clearest evidence is the finding of Jensen and Miller (2008) for rice among very poor households in China.

15.2 Worked examples

Seven bundles the buyer cannot choose between.

\(x\) 10 12 15 20 30 40 60
\(y = 600/x\) 60 50 40 30 20 15 10
\(xy\) 600 600 600 600 600 600 600

Only \(x\) is given in the table. The second row divides 600 by it, and the third row multiplies the two back together. Every column returns 600 (Figure 15.1). So the buyer is indifferent between all seven bundles, and each one delivers \(U = \sqrt{600}\).

The curve is symmetric. Both \((10,60)\) and \((60,10)\) lie on it, and so do \((12,50)\) and \((50,12)\), although the table lists only the first of that pair. Symmetry is a feature of \(xy = k\), because swapping the two coordinates leaves the product alone. In economic terms, the table says that this buyer would exchange \((10,60)\) for \((20,30)\) without hesitating and without gaining. At those quantities, giving up thirty units of \(y\) for ten units of \(x\) is an even swap.

Every affordable bundle, evaluated.

\(x\) 8 12 16 20 24 28 32
\(y = 60 - 1.5x\) 48 42 36 30 24 18 12
\(xy\) 384 504 576 600 576 504 384

The column climbs until \(x = 20\) and then falls away symmetrically. So the best affordable bundle is \((20,30)\), and we used no calculus to find it.

The symmetry is not an accident of the numbers. Substituting gives \(xy = x(60 - 1.5x) = 60x - 1.5x^{2}\). This is a downward parabola in \(x\), whose roots are \(x = 0\) and \(x = 40\), so its peak lies halfway between them, at \(x = 20\). The table suggests the answer among the bundles that it tries, and the parabola confirms that answer for every point on the line. What this approach cannot do is answer the same question when \(p_x\) changes. For that we need the tangency condition, and the formula in Equation (15.1).

The chosen bundle, and both conditions checked. Putting in the numbers \(m = 120\), \(p_x = 3\) and \(p_y = 2\), \[x^{*} = \frac{120}{2(3)} = 20 , \qquad y^{*} = \frac{120}{2(2)} = 30 .\]

check arithmetic
budget constraint \(3(20) + 2(30) = 60 + 60 = 120\) holds
tangency \(y/x = 30/20 = 1.5\) and \(p_x/p_y = 3/2 = 1.5\) holds

This is the same bundle that the table picked out, so three routes agree. The table computed \(xy\) at seven bundles. The check above showed that the bundle is a tangency. And Equation (15.1) returns \((20,30)\). The first route required no theory, and the third requires no arithmetic.

The quantity chosen at six prices.

\(p_x\) $1 $2 $3 $4 $5 $6
\(x^{*} = 60/p_x\) 60 30 20 15 12 10
spent on \(x\), \(p_x x^{*}\) $60 $60 $60 $60 $60 $60

Every entry in the middle row is 60 divided by the price above it. The bottom row never changes, which shows the constant half-share that we proved above.

Two of these columns are already familiar. At \(p_x = \$3\) the quantity is 20, which is bundle \(A\). At \(p_x = \$6\) it is 10, which is bundle \(B\). The two problems we solved at length above are two entries in the table, and the other four came for free (Figure 15.2). In every column, quantity falls as price rises. Here that is not an assumption imported from elsewhere. It is simply what \(60/p_x\) does.
