4  Elasticity

4.1 Definitions

Elasticity. The percentage change in one variable divided by the percentage change in another variable. Because the numerator and the denominator are both percentages, the units cancel and the ratio is a pure number. It has no cups in it, no dollars and no bushels.

For example, a slope of 10 in the coffee market means thousands of cups per dollar, so it cannot be put beside a slope from the housing market. An elasticity of 1.80 can be compared across markets, because the units have gone.

Price elasticity of demand. \[E_{d} = \frac{\%\,\Delta Q^{d}}{\%\,\Delta P} .\] Quantity goes in the numerator and price goes in the denominator. If you swap them, you are answering a different question.

On the coffee curve, a price change of 22.2 percent goes with a quantity change of 40 percent, so \(|E_{d}| = 40.0/22.2 = 1.80\). The law of demand makes the sign negative every time, and for that reason we report the absolute value.

The vocabulary.

Condition Term Buyers …
\(|E_{d}| > 1\) elastic respond more than proportionately
\(|E_{d}| = 1\) unit elastic respond exactly in proportion
\(|E_{d}| < 1\) inelastic respond less than proportionately
\(|E_{d}| = 0\) perfectly inelastic do not respond at all
\(|E_{d}| \to \infty\) perfectly elastic flee any price rise entirely

For example, when coffee is $4 the elasticity is 1.33, so demand at that price is elastic. At $2 it is 0.40, and demand is inelastic.

Point elasticity of demand. The price elasticity of demand, evaluated at a single point on a straight-line demand curve \(Q^{d} = a - bP\), \[|E_{d}| = \left| \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} \cdot \frac{P}{Q} \right| = b\,\frac{P}{Q} .\] Along a straight line, \(\Delta Q / \Delta P = -b\) at every point, and for a price change of any size. That means the measure is exact.

The coffee curve is \(Q^{d} = 70 - 10P\), so \(b = 10\). At a price of $4.50 the quantity is 25, and the point elasticity is \(10 \times 4.50/25 = 1.80\).

Arc elasticity of demand. The price elasticity of demand, evaluated at the averages of two points on a straight-line demand curve \(Q^{d} = a - bP\), \[|E_{d}| = \left| \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} \cdot \frac{\bar{P}}{\bar{Q}} \right| = b\,\frac{\bar{P}}{\bar{Q}} .\]

Here \(\bar{P}\) and \(\bar{Q}\) are the averages of the two prices and of the two quantities. Take the coffee prices of $5 and $4, where the quantities are 20 and 30. The averages are 4.50 and 25, so the arc elasticity is \(10 \times 4.50/25 = 1.80\).

The two extremes. Perfectly inelastic, the vertical case. For example, a transplant patient will buy the prescribed dose of anti-rejection medication at very nearly any price. There is no substitute for the medication, and there is no option of taking less. So a change in price moves the quantity bought very little, and demand comes close to the vertical case.

Perfectly elastic, the horizontal case. Consider one wheat farmer among thousands, selling wheat identical to everyone else’s. If the farmer charges a cent above the going price, every buyer goes elsewhere. If the farmer charges a cent below it, the farmer is simply giving money away.

On the diagram, the first case is a vertical demand curve, with \(|E_{d}| = 0\). The second is a horizontal one, where \(|E_{d}| \to \infty\).

The five determinants.

  1. Close substitutes. Butter’s price rises and buyers switch to margarine; eggs have no stand-in, so egg buyers stay. More substitutes mean more elasticity.

  2. How the market is drawn. Food has no substitute and its demand is inelastic. One bakery’s sourdough loaf has a substitute in every other bakery and its demand is highly elastic. The narrower the market, the more elastic the demand.

  3. Necessity or luxury. A doctor’s visit happens at almost any price; a sailboat does not.

  4. Share of income. Rent doubling rearranges a life; salt doubling goes unnoticed. A bigger budget share makes demand more elastic.

  5. Time to adjust. Gasoline jumps and this month the driving to work continues. Over years come a smaller car, a carpool, a move closer in.

The estimated elasticities show the determinants at work. Eggs, at 0.1, have no close substitute and take a small share of the budget, while restaurant meals, at 2.3, have substitutes everywhere.

Total revenue. The total amount that buyers pay and that sellers receive, \(TR = P \times Q\). On the diagram, total revenue is a rectangle whose height is the price and whose width is the quantity. So revenue is not an extra concept attached to supply and demand. It is an area that has been inside the picture all along.

For example, at the coffee equilibrium the price is $4 and the quantity is 30 thousand cups, so \(TR = 4 \times 30 = \$120\) thousand per week.

Elasticity and total revenue. Suppose the price rises. The price effect is that each unit now sells for more, and that pushes \(TR\) up. The quantity effect is that fewer units are sold, and that pushes \(TR\) down.

Which of the effects wins? The elasticity settles that, because it measures exactly what is in dispute: whether quantity moves proportionately more or less than price.

Price rises Price falls
Elastic (\(|E_{d}|>1\)) \(TR\) falls \(TR\) rises
Unit elastic (\(|E_{d}|=1\)) \(TR\) unchanged \(TR\) unchanged
Inelastic (\(|E_{d}|<1\)) \(TR\) rises \(TR\) falls

When demand is elastic, it is the quantity effect that wins. When demand is inelastic, it is the price effect.

Suppose 100 units sell at $10, and the price rises by 20 percent, to $12. If \(|E_{d}| = 2\), quantity falls to 60 and revenue falls from $1,000 to $720. If \(|E_{d}| = 0.5\), quantity falls only to 90, and revenue rises to $1,080.

Price elasticity of supply. \[E_{s} = \frac{\%\,\Delta Q^{s}}{\%\,\Delta P} .\] Because supply slopes upward, both changes have the same sign, and so \(E_{s}\) comes out positive on its own, with no need for an absolute value. The vocabulary carries over unchanged. Supply is elastic above one, unit elastic at one and inelastic below one, and the same two extremes sit at the ends.

Suppose a bakery supplies 3,000 loaves a week at $3 a loaf, and 5,000 loaves at $4. By the midpoint method, quantity changes by 50 percent and price by 28.6 percent, so \(E_{s} = 1.75\) and supply is elastic.

The hard limit. Some quantities are fixed by nature rather than by cost. No price, however high, will create more coastline. So the physical supply of beachfront land is perfectly inelastic, \(E_{s} = 0\). This is the one constraint that no amount of time will relax.

At the other extreme is \(E_{s} \to \infty\), which is a horizontal supply curve. At the going price, the quantity supplied responds without limit.

On the diagram, the supply of beachfront land is a vertical line, with the same quantity at every price. The other extreme is a horizontal line at the going price.

Tax incidence. Who actually bears the burden of a tax, as opposed to who is legally required to hand the money over. The person who writes the check is not necessarily the person who pays.

For example, take a $1 tax in a market where \(|E_{d}| = 0.43\) and \(E_{s} = 1.71\). Buyers pay $6.80, up from $6.00, and sellers keep $5.80, so buyers bear 80 cents of every dollar of tax and sellers bear 20 cents.

4.2 Worked examples

One line, seven elasticities. \(Q^{d} = 70 - 10P\), which has a slope of 10 everywhere.

\(P\) ($) \(Q\) (000s) \(10 \times P/Q\) \(|E_{d}|\) Verdict
6 10 \(10 \times 6/10\) 6.00 elastic
5 20 \(10 \times 5/20\) 2.50 elastic
4 30 \(10 \times 4/30\) 1.33 elastic
3.50 35 \(10 \times 3.5/35\) 1.00 unit elastic
3 40 \(10 \times 3/40\) 0.75 inelastic
2 50 \(10 \times 2/50\) 0.40 inelastic
1 60 \(10 \times 1/60\) 0.17 inelastic

The slope is 10 in every row, and that is what it means for a line to be straight. The only thing that changes is the fraction \(P/Q\), but it changes a great deal. As you move up the curve, \(P\) rises while \(Q\) falls, so the ratio is pushed from both ends at once. Going from \(1/60\) to \(6/10\) is a factor of thirty-six, and yet the slope of the line never moved.
