2  Gains from Trade

2.1 Definitions

Specialization and exchange. Agents concentrate their production on a narrow range of goods and get the rest by exchange. Nobody produces everything they consume.

Consider Smith’s pin workshop, where ten men who divide the work between them make upwards of forty-eight thousand pins in a day. A man who makes only pins must get his food, clothing and shelter from others, so he specializes and he exchanges.

Absolute advantage. Producing a good with fewer inputs than another producer does. It is a direct comparison of productivities.

Suppose a unit of cloth takes 100 hours of labor in England and 90 in Portugal, and a unit of wine takes 120 hours in England and 80 in Portugal. Portugal needs fewer hours for both goods, so it has the absolute advantage in both.

Comparative advantage. Producing a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer does.

A unit of wine costs Portugal 0.89 units of cloth and costs England 1.20, so Portugal has the comparative advantage in wine. A unit of cloth costs England 0.83 units of wine and costs Portugal 1.125, so England has the comparative advantage in cloth, even though it needs more hours than Portugal for both goods.

Autarky. The absence of trade. A country consumes precisely what it produces, so its production frontier is also its consumption frontier.

Suppose England puts 1,800 hours into each good. It then makes 18 units of cloth and 15 of wine, and under autarky that is exactly what it consumes.

Specialization raises world output. Now suppose all of each country’s labor moves into the good in which that country holds the comparative advantage. England then produces \(3600/100 = 36\) units of cloth and no wine. Portugal produces \(2880/80 = 36\) units of wine and no cloth. World output is now 36 units of cloth and 36 of wine, against 34 and 33 under autarky.

Here England has 3,600 hours of labor and Portugal 2,880, and the autarky figures suppose that each country splits its hours equally between the two goods. So world output rises by two units of cloth and three of wine, and no hour of labor has been added.

Terms of trade. The rate at which the two goods exchange once trade opens. Let \(p\) be the price of one unit of wine in units of cloth. There is no money in the model, so a price is a ratio between goods.

Portugal sells wine, and it will not accept less than its own cost of producing wine. That requires \(p > \tfrac{8}{9} \approx 0.89\). England buys wine, and it will not pay more than its own cost of producing wine, which requires \(p < 1.20\). Both conditions hold only within \[\tfrac{8}{9} < p < 1.20 .\]

Suppose 17 units of wine trade for 17 units of cloth, so that \(p = 1\). That price is above Portugal’s cost of 0.89 units of cloth and below England’s cost of 1.20, so both countries accept it.

Consumption beyond the frontier. With trade, a country’s consumption point may lie outside its own production frontier. Production is still bounded by the frontier. Consumption is bounded by what the country produces together with what that production can be exchanged for.

After trade England consumes 19 units of cloth and 17 of wine. Its own frontier allows only 15.6 units of cloth at 17 units of wine, so England is consuming a bundle that it could not have produced.

2.2 Worked examples

Hours of labor required per unit.

Cloth Wine
England 100 120
Portugal 90 80

Each entry is a cost, so a larger number means worse performance. Portugal needs fewer hours than England for cloth, 90 against 100. It also needs fewer for wine, 80 against 120. So Portugal is the more productive country in both goods.

Ricardo states these requirements as the labor of a given number of men for one year. Here they are read as hours. That changes nothing, because only the ratios between the four figures do any work.

Ricardo gave labor requirements but did not say how much labor each country had. Labor requirements fix the slope of a frontier but not its position, so the two endowments must be supplied here: England 3,600 hours and Portugal 2,880. These may be read as ten workers against eight. The figures are chosen so that the outputs derived below come out as whole numbers. The pattern of comparative advantage does not depend on them, although the output figures do.

The two frontiers. Labor put into cloth plus labor put into wine uses up the whole available supply. For England, \[100C + 120W = 3600 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C = 36 - 1.2\,W .\] And for Portugal, \[90C + 80W = 2880 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C = 32 - \tfrac{8}{9}\,W .\] So England can produce at most 36 units of cloth or 30 of wine. For Portugal the limits are 32 units of cloth or 36 of wine.

Both frontiers are straight lines. The slope of each one is that country’s opportunity cost of wine, measured in cloth.

Opportunity costs derived for both countries. Start with England. One unit of wine takes 120 hours. Those 120 hours would otherwise have produced \(120/100 = 1.2\) units of cloth. Therefore \[\text{England: 1 wine costs } \tfrac{120}{100} = 1.20 \text{ cloth} .\] Now run the same calculation the other way. One unit of cloth takes 100 hours, and those hours would otherwise have produced \(100/120\) units of wine: \[\text{England: 1 cloth costs } \tfrac{100}{120} \approx 0.83 \text{ wine} .\] For Portugal, one unit of wine takes 80 hours, which would otherwise have produced \(80/90\) units of cloth. One unit of cloth takes 90 hours, which would otherwise have produced \(90/80\) units of wine: \[\text{Portugal: 1 wine costs } \tfrac{80}{90} \approx 0.89 \text{ cloth} , \qquad \text{1 cloth costs } \tfrac{90}{80} = 1.125 \text{ wine} .\]

The two entries in each row are reciprocals, since \(1.20 \times 0.83 \approx 1\) and \(0.89 \times 1.125 \approx 1\). That is arithmetic rather than coincidence. With two goods and one resource, the cost of cloth in terms of wine and the cost of wine in terms of cloth are two readings of a single ratio. So a country that finds one good expensive necessarily finds the other cheap.

The wine column reproduces the slopes of the two frontiers in Figure 2.1, 1.20 and \(\tfrac{8}{9}\). Again this is arithmetic, since the slope of a frontier is an opportunity cost. The cloth column holds their reciprocals.

We need a baseline against which a gain can be measured. Suppose each country splits its labor equally between the two goods: England puts 1,800 hours into each, and Portugal 1,440 into each. This split is assumed rather than derived. The pattern of comparative advantage does not depend on it, although the size of the gains reported below does.

Autarky output.

Cloth Wine
England \(1800/100 = 18\) \(1800/120 = 15\)
Portugal \(1440/90 = 16\) \(1440/80 = 18\)
World 34 33

England may be checked against its frontier, \(36 - 1.2(15) = 18\), and Portugal against its own, \(32 - \tfrac{8}{9}(18) = 16\). Both countries are on their frontiers, since each uses all of its labor. Gains to the world are measured against 34 units of cloth and 33 of wine. Gains to each country are measured against its own row.

Set \(p = 1\), so one unit of cloth trades for one of wine. That price lies inside the band, and it gives whole numbers.

The volume exchanged. Let \(X\) be the number of units exchanged. England keeps \(36 - X\) units of cloth and receives \(X\) units of wine. Portugal keeps \(36 - X\) units of wine and receives \(X\) units of cloth.

England improves on autarky if \(36 - X > 18\) and \(X > 15\), which requires \(15 < X < 18\). Portugal improves on autarky if \(X > 16\) and \(36 - X > 18\), which requires \(16 < X < 18\). So both conditions hold when \(16 < X < 18\). Take \(X = 17\).

The outcome.

Cloth made Cloth consumed Wine made Wine consumed
England 36 \(36-17=19\) 0 \(0+17=17\)
Portugal 0 \(0+17=17\) 36 \(36-17=19\)

Before trade, England had 18 units of cloth and 15 of wine. Now it has 19 and 17 (Figure 2.2). Portugal had 16 and 18, and now has 17 and 19. Each country has more of both goods, and neither has worked a single extra hour.
