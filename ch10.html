10  Production and Costs

10.1 Definitions

Profit. Total revenue minus total cost, written \(\pi = TR - TC\), where \(TR = P \times Q\).

Consider an engineer who works as an independent consultant and bills $280,000 in a year. Once all of her costs are counted, they come to $210,000, so her profit is $70,000.

Explicit and implicit costs. Explicit costs are input costs that require a direct money payment, such as wages, rent, materials and the interest on a loan. Implicit costs are input costs that involve no money payment. They are the value of what the owner gives up, for example the salary not earned elsewhere and the interest the invested savings could have earned.

The engineer pays $30,000 in office rent and $20,000 for equipment, and both are explicit costs. The $150,000 salary she gave up to work for herself is an implicit cost, and so is the $10,000 of interest that her $200,000 of savings no longer earns at 5 percent.

Accounting profit and economic profit. Accounting profit is total revenue minus explicit costs. Economic profit is total revenue minus all opportunity costs, both explicit and implicit.

On revenue of $280,000, the engineer’s accounting profit is \(280{,}000 - 50{,}000 = \$230{,}000\), and her economic profit is \(280{,}000 - 210{,}000 = \$70{,}000\). The gap between the two is $160,000, which is exactly her implicit costs, \(150{,}000 + 10{,}000\).

Short run and long run. In the short run at least one input is fixed. For example, the firm can hire workers at short notice, but it cannot build a bigger factory at short notice. In the long run all inputs are variable, including the size of the factory.

A food truck can change everything about its operation in perhaps six weeks, while a nuclear plant may need a decade. So the long run is not a number of months. It is however long the fixed input takes to become a matter of choice.

Production function. The relationship between the inputs a firm uses and the output it produces, written here as \(Q = f(L)\).

Take a firm that assembles electronic components, with its machines and floor space fixed and \(L\) workers per shift. Its production function is

\[ Q = 24L - L^{2} , \qquad 0 \le L \le 12 , \tag{10.1}\]

with \(Q\) measured in components per shift, so 3 workers produce \(72 - 9 = 63\) components.

Marginal product. The additional output produced by one more unit of an input, holding the other inputs fixed: \(MP = \Delta Q / \Delta L\).

The assembler makes 63 components with 3 workers and 80 with 4. The marginal product of the fourth worker is therefore \(80 - 63 = 17\) components.

Diminishing marginal product. As more and more of a variable input is added, with the other inputs held fixed, the marginal product of that input eventually falls.

The assembler’s first worker adds 23 components and the twelfth adds only 1. The twelfth worker is no less capable than the first. She adds less because the same machines are now shared among 12 people.

Marginal cost is the wage divided by marginal product. Hiring one more worker costs the wage \(w\) and yields \(MP\) extra units of output, so each of those units costs \[MC = \frac{w}{MP} .\]

The assembler pays $50 for a worker-shift. The first worker adds 23 components, so each of them costs \(50/23 = \$2.17\). The ninth worker adds 7, so each of those costs \(50/7 = \$7.14\), although the wage has not changed.

Fixed and variable costs. Fixed costs, \(FC\), do not vary with output, and they are owed even at \(Q = 0\). For the workshop, \(FC = 100\). Variable costs, \(VC\), vary with output. In this case \(VC = 60Q - 12Q^{2} + Q^{3}\). Every dollar of total cost is one or the other, so \(TC = FC + VC\).

The workshop here builds oak chairs, and its daily total cost in dollars for \(Q\) chairs is \(TC = 100 + 60Q - 12Q^{2} + Q^{3}\). At 6 chairs a day total cost is $244, of which $100 is fixed and \(244 - 100 = \$144\) is variable.

The three average costs. Average fixed cost is \(AFC = FC/Q\), which is the fixed bill spread over the units produced. Average variable cost is \(AVC = VC/Q\), the variable bill per unit. Average total cost is \(ATC = TC/Q\), which is what the typical unit costs. Since \(TC = FC + VC\), dividing through by \(Q\) gives \(ATC = AFC + AVC\).

At 6 chairs a day the workshop has \(AFC = 100/6 = \$16.67\), \(AVC = 144/6 = \$24.00\) and \(ATC = 244/6 = \$40.67\). We can check the identity on these numbers, since \(16.67 + 24.00 = 40.67\).

Marginal cost. The increase in total cost that comes from producing one more unit, \(MC = \Delta TC / \Delta Q\).

The workshop’s total cost is $225 at 5 chairs and $244 at 6, so the marginal cost of the sixth chair is \(244 - 225 = \$19\). None of the fixed $100 is in that figure, because the $100 was owed anyway.

The pulling principle. The cost of each new unit is a new mark entering the average. If \(MC < ATC\), the new unit is cheap and pulls the average down. If \(MC > ATC\), the newcomer is expensive and drags the average up. If \(MC = ATC\), the average stands still.

As long as \(MC\) lies below \(ATC\), average total cost is falling. Once \(MC\) lies above \(ATC\), average total cost is rising. A quantity that falls and then rises has its minimum at the changeover. In a table the changeover falls between two rows, where \(MC\) passes from below \(ATC\) to above it. On smooth cost curves it is the point where \(MC = ATC\) exactly. Marginal cost therefore crosses average total cost at the minimum of average total cost.

The workshop’s seventh chair costs $31 and joins an average of $40.67, so the average falls to $39.29. The eighth chair costs $49, which is more than $39.29, and the average rises to $40.50.

Efficient scale. The quantity of output at which average total cost is lowest.

For the chair workshop the efficient scale is 7 chairs a day, where average total cost is $39.29. This is not necessarily the output the workshop should choose, because profit depends on price as well as on cost.

Long-run average cost. The lowest average total cost attainable at each level of output when the firm is free to choose its factory. Geometrically, it is the lower envelope of the short-run average total cost curves.

Suppose a firm can build a small, a medium or a large plant. The small plant is the cheapest per unit when orders are thin and the large plant when volume is high, and the long-run curve takes whichever is lowest at each output.

Economies and diseconomies of scale. Economies of scale occur where \(LRAC\) falls as output rises. Constant returns to scale occur where \(LRAC\) is flat. Diseconomies of scale occur where \(LRAC\) rises as output rises.

Adam Smith’s pin factory of ten workers split the making of a pin into distinct operations. It produced tens of thousands of pins a day, where a lone artisan performing every step himself could scarcely make 20. That is an economy of scale. Diseconomies arrive later, when management layers multiply and the cost of coordination rises faster than output.

10.2 Worked examples

A baker who leaves salaried work. Here is a second example. Nadia leaves a $70,000 job as a staff baker to open her own bakery. Each year she pays rent of $24,000, ingredients and utilities of $36,000, and $20,000 for a part-time assistant. She also takes $50,000 of savings, which had been earning 4 percent, and puts it into ovens. Her revenue is $160,000.

Add up the explicit costs first: \(24{,}000 + 36{,}000 + 20{,}000 = \$80{,}000\). The implicit costs are the salary she gave up plus the interest her savings stopped earning, \(70{,}000 + 0.04 \times 50{,}000 = \$72{,}000\). So her total opportunity cost is $152,000.

Accounting Economic
Revenue 160,000 160,000
Costs 80,000 152,000
Profit 80,000 8,000

It is economic profit that decides whether she stays, because it is the figure that compares the bakery with the life she gave up. She is beating that life by $8,000. If her revenue were $152,000, she would be exactly indifferent.

Choosing the factory. The per-unit cost of each factory, read off the three curves, is as follows:

A firm that plans on 2 units a day builds small. One that plans on 6 builds medium, and one that plans on 10 builds large. At two units a day the large plant costs over $23 per unit, because it is mostly idle and its rent is spread over almost nothing. At ten units the small plant costs over $23 a unit, for the opposite reason. It has been pushed far past its capacity, and crowding has taken over. So the two mechanisms behind these high costs are quite different.
