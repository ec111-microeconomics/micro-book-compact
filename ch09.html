9  Public Choice

9.1 Definitions

Rent created by a restriction. The price minus the marginal cost at the restricted quantity, multiplied by that quantity. The rent is paid to whoever holds the right to sell, and it exists only because the restriction exists.

Take the coffee market of Chapter 6 under a cap of 20 thousand cups a week. Buyers pay $5 a cup and the twenty-thousandth cup costs $3 to produce, so the rent is \((5 - 3) \times 20 = 40\), or $40,000 a week. It is the rectangle in Figure 9.1.

Collective action problem. The failure of a group of people to act in their common interest.

Suppose 10 sellers hold the licenses, with $78,000 a year at stake each, and 10,000 buyers purchase the coffee, with $130 a year at stake each. So a holder can gain by paying for a campaign to keep the cap, and a buyer cannot gain by paying for one to remove it, even though the buyers lose more as a group.

Rational ignorance. Choosing to stay uninformed about a policy because the expected benefit of learning about it is less than the cost of learning.

Consider a buyer with $130 a year at stake and a 1 in 100,000 chance of casting the decisive vote. The expected benefit of learning about the cap is $0.0013, and learning takes an hour valued at $20, so the buyer stays uninformed.

Rent-seeking. Spending real resources to obtain a transfer that a policy has created. The spending determines who gets the transfer, and it adds nothing to output.

Suppose the council will issue 10 licenses, each a right to sell 2,000 cups a week and each worth $4,000 a week. If applicants spend the full $40,000 on lawyers and consultants to win them, the cap costs society $50,000 a week and not $10,000, as Figure 9.2 shows.

Regulatory capture. The decisions of a regulator come to serve the industry that it regulates.

A licensing board could raise the cap to 30 thousand cups. Each holder would then lose $1,500 a week and each buyer would gain $2.50, so the arguments the board hears about the size of the cap come mainly from the holders.

Who gains and who loses from the cap. The equilibrium values are the ones found in Chapter 6. Consumer surplus under the cap is the figure that Chapter 6 found at a price of $5.

The fall in total surplus is the deadweight loss of $10,000 from Chapter 6, which we have now reached by a second route.

The stake of each member.

Members Group, weekly Each, weekly Each, yearly
License holders 10 \(+\$15{,}000\) \(+\$1{,}500\) \(+\$78{,}000\)
Buyers 10,000 \(-\$25{,}000\) \(-\$2.50\) \(-\$130\)

Each row takes the group’s change, divides it by the number of members in the group, and multiplies by 52 weeks: \(15{,}000/10 = 1{,}500\) and \(1{,}500 \times 52 = 78{,}000\) for holders, and \(25{,}000/10{,}000 = 2.50\) and \(2.50 \times 52 = 130\) for buyers. Taken as a group, buyers lose more than holders gain, since $25,000 is more than $15,000. Per person, the comparison runs the other way. Each holder gains \(1{,}500/2.50 = 600\) times what each buyer loses.

India, 1964. The table shows the value of rents from five sources, in millions of rupees, as Krueger (1974, pp. 293–294) estimated them.

Source of rent Rs million
Public investment 365
Imports 10,271
Controlled commodities 3,000
Credit rationing 407
Railways 602
Total \(365 + 10{,}271 + 3{,}000 + 407 + 602 = 14{,}645\)

National income in 1964 was Rs 201 billion. So rents were \(14{,}645/201{,}000 = 7.3\) percent of national income. The largest source was import licenses. Krueger valued them at 75 percent of the value of 1964 imports. That is a conservative figure, since the government’s Santhanam Committee reported that licenses were worth between 100 and 500 percent of their face value. Investment licensing is left out, because there is no basis on which to value it.

Turkey, 1968. Krueger (1974, p. 294) bases the estimate on a sample of imports that covers about 10 percent of the 1968 total, with values in millions of Turkish lira. At the border, the sample cost TL 547 million. Once duties, taxes and landing charges were added, it cost TL 1,443 million. At wholesale it sold for TL 3,568 million. Wholesaling is work too, so allow a markup of 50 percent on landed cost for it. Then the value of the licenses is \[3{,}568 - 1.5 \times 1{,}443 = 3{,}568 - 2{,}164.5 = 1{,}403.5 ,\] which Krueger rounds to TL 1,404 million. That is \(1{,}404/547 = 2.57\) times the border value of the imports, a ratio that Krueger describes as almost three times. Now convert this into a share of national income. Imports at border prices were 6 percent of national income. So license rents were about \(2.57 \times 6 = 15.4\) percent, which Krueger reports as about 15 percent of GNP.
