8  Public Goods and Common Resources

8.1 Definitions

Excludable. A good is excludable if a supplier can stop people who have not paid for it from using it.

A burrito is excludable, because if there is no payment there is no burrito. Street lighting is not excludable, since the light that falls on a paying pedestrian falls just as much on the next one.

Rival in use. A good is rival in use if one person’s use of a unit reduces what is left over for everybody else.

A fish, once landed, cannot be landed a second time, so fish are rival in use. A streamed show is not rival, because one person’s viewing of it takes nothing away from anyone else’s.

Public good. A good that is neither excludable nor rival. Nobody can be kept out, and a single supply serves everybody at once.

The mosquito treatment program at Alder Pond is a public good. A licensed crew treats the pond for $2,500 a season, and all 120 households around it get the benefit whether they pay or not.

Free rider. A person who enjoys the benefit of a good without paying for it.

The neighborhood association asks each of the 120 households around Alder Pond for $25. Take a household that values a mosquito-free season at $50. If it keeps its $25 and the program runs, it is a free rider, because the treatment reaches its yard either way.

Cost-benefit analysis. Comparing the total benefit of a project, to everyone it affects, with its total cost, in order to decide whether the project is worth doing.

For the Alder Pond program, the total benefit to the 120 households is $3,300 a season and the total cost is $2,500. The benefit is larger by $800, so the program is worth doing.

Common resource. A good that is rival in use but not excludable. Anyone may take from it, and every taking leaves less for the rest.

Birch Lake is a common resource, because it is open to anyone with a boat. Catch per boat is \(1200 - 100\,b\) dollars of fish a day, where \(b\) is the number of boats on the water, so every boat already fishing loses $100 of catch when one more boat launches.

The tragedy of the commons. A user weighs the full private gain against the fraction of the true cost that lands on that user. Take any use whose full cost to everyone exceeds its gain, but whose private share of that cost does not. That use gets undertaken, and those are exactly the uses that should not happen.

A boat-day on Birch Lake costs $400. The fifth boat lands $700 of fish, so it launches, but $400 of that catch is fish the other four boats would have landed anyway.

Rent dissipation. The rent of a resource is the value that the resource itself generates over and above the cost of working it. Under open access, entry continues until every user exactly breaks even. A fleet in which every boat breaks even generates no net value at all. The rent is fully dissipated.

On Birch Lake a fleet of four boats earns a net $1,600 a day. Under open access eight boats launch, each one catches $400 against a cost of $400, and the net is $0.

8.2 Worked examples

The two columns a household faces.

program runs anyway program fails anyway
contribute $25 \(50 - 25 = \$25\) \(-\$25\)
keep the $25 \(\$50\) \(\$0\)

Keeping the money is better in both columns. Contributing pays only in the one case the table leaves out, where the fund stands at exactly $2,475 without this check, and among 120 households that case is a knife edge. A choice that is best whatever everyone else does is called a dominant choice. Here keeping the money is dominant everywhere except on that knife edge. So the household barely needs to predict what its neighbors will do.

The Alder Pond program, valued. The 120 households do not value the program equally. Forty of them sit on the pond, and each would pay up to $50 for a calm summer. Another 60 are set back from the water and would pay up to $20 each. The last 20 are on the far ridge, where the mosquitoes barely reach, and would pay up to $5.

group benefit running total
40 households \(\times\) $50 \(= \$2{,}000\) $2,000
60 households \(\times\) $20 \(= \$1{,}200\) $3,200
20 households \(\times\) $5 \(= \$100\) $3,300
total benefit \(B\) $3,300
total cost \(C\) $2,500
net gain \(B - C\) \(3{,}300 - 2{,}500 = \$800\)

So the town is $800 a season richer with the program than without it, and the test says build it.

Total catch climbs to $3,600 at six boats and then falls, because crowding eventually outweighs the extra headcount. From the seventh boat onward the extra catch is negative, so the fleet lands less fish than it would with one boat fewer.

The net column peaks at four boats and $1,600 a day. At six boats, where total catch peaks, net is still $1,200. So maximizing the catch is not the same as maximizing the value, because catching fish costs money. Net reaches exactly zero at eight boats, and that figure comes back later.

Entry stops at four boats. The two routes agree, and they had to. The net column rises exactly as long as each added boat brings in more than it costs, and it falls once it does not. So the peak of net and the last boat worth adding are one fact, read two ways. That is what makes the agreement a useful check: two computations that must agree will differ only if one of them contains an error.

Entry under open access.

boat catch per boat against $400 decision
6th $600 \(600 > 400\) launch

9th $300 \(300 < 400\) stay ashore

Entry stops at eight boats, which is twice the four boats that maximize the lake’s total profit.
