11  Firms in Competitive Markets

11.1 Definitions

Four ways a market can be organized.

Structure Sellers Product Power over price
Perfect competition many identical none
Monopolistic competition many differentiated a little
Oligopoly few either often considerable
Monopoly one unique the most

The fewer the sellers and the more distinctive the product, the more each firm can typically say about its own price.

The running example of this chapter sits in the top row. It is a maker of standardized electronic connectors, one of 120 identical firms, with total cost \(TC = 144 + 36q - 8q^{2} + q^{3}\) dollars a day, where \(q\) is one firm’s output and \(Q\) is the output of the whole market.

Competitive market. A market with many buyers and many sellers, in which the goods are identical across sellers and there is free entry and exit.

The connector market meets all three conditions. One supplier’s output is indistinguishable from another’s, no plant is big enough to matter on its own, and anyone may open a plant or close one.

Price taker. A buyer or seller who takes the market price as given and cannot influence it.

Suppose the market price of a connector is $48. A firm that charges $49 loses every buyer to a rival, and a firm that charges $47 wins no extra sales, because it could already sell its whole output at $48. It simply hands a dollar to each buyer.

Average and marginal revenue. Average revenue is the revenue per unit sold, \(AR = TR/q\). Marginal revenue is the change in total revenue when the firm sells one more unit, \(MR = \Delta TR\).

At $48 the connector firm’s revenue is \(TR = 48q\), so a seventh connector raises revenue from $288 to $336, and \(MR = 336 - 288 = \$48\). Marginal revenue equals the price because the firm never has to cut its price to sell more.

The output rule. If \(MR > MC\), the next unit brings in more than it costs, so making it raises profit, and the firm expands. If \(MR < MC\), the last unit cost more than it brought in, so cutting it raises profit, and the firm cuts back. So profit is greatest at the last unit whose \(MR\) still covers its \(MC\). When output can be varied in fractions, that point is exactly where \(MR = MC\). The rule holds for every firm that maximizes profit. For a price taker, \(MR = P\), so the rule becomes \(P = MC\).

At $48 the sixth connector costs $39 to make and the seventh costs $59. So the firm makes the sixth, skips the seventh, and produces \(q^{*} = 6\).

Constructing the profit rectangle. First find the output, where the price line meets marginal cost. Then read average total cost at that same output, not at its minimum. The vertical gap between the price line and the \(ATC\) curve is profit per unit. The rectangle runs from zero to \(q^{*}\), with its top at the price line and its bottom at \(ATC\), and its area is profit.

At a price of $71 the firm produces 7, where \(ATC\) is $49.57. The rectangle is \(71 - 49.57 = 21.43\) high and 7 wide, so its area is $150 (Figure 11.1).

The firm’s short-run supply curve. The firm’s short-run supply curve is its marginal cost curve above average variable cost, and zero below it. In whole units the curve rises in steps, and the smooth curve is the version in which output is divisible.

The connector firm supplies nothing at $15, 5 at $31, 6 at $48 and 7 at $71. At exactly $20, the minimum of its average variable cost, it is indifferent between producing 4 and producing nothing.

Exit. Leaving the market permanently. The plant is sold and the lease ends, so the fixed costs stop too.

A connector firm that exits stops paying its fixed bill of $144 a day. A firm that merely shuts down keeps paying it and waits for the price to recover.

Two thresholds, two runs. In the short run the fixed cost is sunk, so the test compares the price with the minimum of \(AVC\). In the long run no future cost is fixed, because the firm can avoid every cost it has still to pay by leaving, so the test becomes the price against the minimum of \(ATC\).

Price Short run Long run
\(P < 20\) shut down exit
\(20 \le P < 48\) operate at a loss exit
\(P = 48\) operate, \(\pi = 0\) stay, and no one enters
\(P > 48\) operate, \(\pi > 0\) stay, and entrants come

At a price of $31 the connector firm makes 5 and loses $94 a day, but $31 is above its average variable cost of $21 at that output, so it operates today. It also arranges to leave when the lease expires, because $31 does not cover all of its costs.

Entry and exit. In the long run the number of firms is not fixed, and it is profit that changes it. A profit draws in entrants, which shifts market supply to the right and pushes the price down. A loss drives firms out, which shifts supply to the left and pushes the price up. In the short run a shift in demand moves the price. In the long run it moves the number of firms.

When connector demand rises, the price first spikes to $71 and then falls back to $48.20 as 58 new firms enter, so the industry grows from 120 firms to 178 (Stages 1 to 3 below).

Long-run competitive equilibrium. The state in which \(P = MC\), so that each firm maximizes profit; \(P\) equals the minimum of \(ATC\), so that economic profit is zero and no firm enters or leaves; and each firm produces at its efficient scale.

The three conditions are one point, because marginal cost crosses average total cost at its minimum (Chapter 10). For the connector firm the point is \(q = 6\) at $48.

Rent to a scarce advantage. A surplus earned by a firm whose costs are below those of the marginal entrant, because entrants cannot replicate the advantage that produces the lower costs. It survives in long-run equilibrium.

Suppose entrants to a farming market face a minimum \(ATC\) of $48 at \(q = 6\), and a few incumbent farms on unusually good land face $40. The price settles at $48, set by the marginal entrant, so a good-land farm earns at least \((48 - 40) \times 6 = \$48\) a day, and entry cannot compete it away because entrants cannot copy the land.

What makes long-run supply flat. The long-run supply curve is horizontal at the minimum of \(ATC\) when input prices are unaffected by the size of the industry and all firms have the same costs. Where input prices rise as the industry grows, or where each further entrant has higher costs than the one before it, the curve slopes upward, and the price settles at whatever covers the marginal entrant’s costs.

Suppose connector plants need a specialized alloy in limited supply. A wave of entry bids up the alloy’s price, every firm’s cost curves shift up, and a bigger market needs a permanently higher price.

Only \(q\) is given, and \(TC\) is the cost function evaluated at that output. Take the row for \(q = 6\) as a check. Total cost is \(TC = 144 + 216 - 288 + 216 = 288\). Subtracting the fixed cost gives \(VC = 288 - 144 = 144\). Dividing by output gives \(AVC = 144/6 = 24.00\) and \(ATC = 288/6 = 48.00\). And for the sixth unit, \(MC = 288 - 249 = 39\). The marginal cost column needs one comment. The entry recorded against \(q\) is the cost of the \(q\)th unit, so it belongs between \(q - 1\) and \(q\). The table puts it in the row for \(q\), and the figures plot it at \(q - \tfrac{1}{2}\).

The firm produces \(q^{*} = 6\). Why is this optimal? Look at the seventh unit. It would bring in $48 of revenue, but it would cost $59 to make. So the firm would lose $11 on it. It does not pay to produce the seventh unit. Now look at it the other way. If the firm stopped at five, it would give up the sixth unit, and the sixth unit adds $9. So the firm stops at six.

Now add up what each of the six units brings in over and above its own cost: \(19 + 29 + 33 + 31 + 23 + 9 = \$144\). Notice that $144 is exactly the fixed cost. In other words, what the firm earns over its running costs pays the fixed bill exactly, with nothing left over. So at a price of $48, economic profit is exactly zero.

A price of $71. At $71 the seventh unit costs $59 and is worth making, but the eighth costs $85 and is not, so \(q^{*} = 7\). Using totals, \[\pi = 71 \times 7 - 347 = 497 - 347 = +\$150 .\] Using the per-unit route instead, \(ATC\) at \(q = 7\) is \(347/7 = \$49.57\), so \[\pi = (P - ATC) \times q = (71 - 49.57) \times 7 = 21.43 \times 7 = \$150 .\] The two routes give the same answer (Figure 11.1).

Operating at a loss on purpose.

operate at \(q = 5\) shut down
revenue 155 0
variable cost 105 0
fixed cost 144 144
result \(-\$94\) \(-\$144\)

Operating is better by $50, and that $50 has a precise meaning. Revenue of $155 covers all $105 of variable cost and leaves $50 to put toward the fixed bill. This is \((31 - 21) \times 5\), the gap between the price and average variable cost on each of the five units. Fifty dollars of the rent paid means fifty dollars less lost.

Stage 1, the market at rest. Demand is \(Q^{d} = 1200 - 10P\). At the zero-profit price of $48, buyers demand \(1200 - 480 = 720\), and 120 firms supply \(120 \times 6 = 720\). So the market balances.

Call this point \(A\): \(P = 48\), \(Q = 720\), 120 firms, each producing \(q = 6\) and earning \(\pi = 0\). Here nothing moves. There is no profit to attract entrants and no loss to drive incumbents out, and each firm is already at its best output for this price.

Stage 2, demand jumps. Now a new technology creates a need for connectors everywhere, and demand shifts to \(Q^{d} = 1550 - 10P\). Buyers now want 350 more at every price. At the old price of $48 they demand \(1550 - 480 = 1070\) against a supply of 720, so there is a shortage of 350, and a shortage bids the price up.

The cast is still frozen at 120 firms, so the price rises along their existing supply schedule until the two sides meet. At $71 buyers demand \(1550 - 710 = 840\), and the firms supply \(120 \times 7 = 840\). Call this point \(B\): \(P = 71\), \(Q = 840\), still with 120 firms.

Each firm has moved up its marginal cost curve from 6 units to 7, because at $71 the seventh unit, which costs $59, is now worth making. The market’s extra 120 units come from existing firms working harder, since there are no others yet. Each firm earns \(71 \times 7 - 347 = +\$150\) a day.

Stage 3, where entry stops. If the price went all the way back to $48, buyers would take \(1550 - 480 = 1070\). Firms at their efficient scale make 6 each, so the market would hold \(1070/6 = 178.33\) firms. A third of a firm cannot enter, so we have to test the whole numbers on either side. In whole units each firm produces 6 at any price between $39 and $59, so \(Q = 6n\) and \(P = (1550 - Q)/10\). Profit per firm is then \(6P - 288\), since \(TC\) at \(q = 6\) is 288.

\(Q = 6n\) \(P = (1550 - Q)/10\) \(\pi = 6P - 288\)
\(n = 178\) 1068 $48.20 \(289.20 - 288 = +\$1.20\)
\(n = 179\) 1074 $47.60 \(285.60 - 288 = -\$2.40\)

The 178th firm still covers all of its costs. A 179th firm would push the price to $47.60 and lose $2.40, so it stays out, and entry stops at 178 firms.
