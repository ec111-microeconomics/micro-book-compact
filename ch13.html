13  Oligopoly

13.1 Definitions

Oligopoly. A market structure in which only a few sellers offer similar or identical products.

Consider a valley town with exactly two ready-mix concrete plants, Ridge Concrete and Vale Concrete, selling an identical product. Market demand is \(P = 60 - Q\), with \(Q\) in yards a day, and each plant produces at a marginal cost of $12 a yard, so each plant’s profit is \((P - 12)\) times the yards it sells.

Strategic interdependence. Each firm’s best choice depends on the choices the other firms make, and every firm knows it.

Suppose Ridge produces \(q\) while Vale produces 12. The price is then \(60 - (12 + q) = 48 - q\), so Ridge’s profit,

\[ (48 - q - 12)\,q = (36 - q)\,q , \tag{13.1}\]

depends on Vale’s output as well as its own.

Concentration ratio. The percentage of a market’s total output supplied by its four largest firms.

If a town’s six grocery stores have market shares of 30, 25, 20, 10, 10 and 5 percent, the four largest supply \(30 + 25 + 20 + 10 = 85\) percent. A market of ten firms with 10 percent each has a ratio of only 40 percent.

Collusion and cartels. Collusion is an agreement among firms in a market about the quantities to produce or the prices to charge. A cartel is a group of firms acting in unison.

If Ridge and Vale collude, they produce the monopoly quantity of 24 yards between them, 12 each, at \(P = 60 - 24 = 36\). Each plant earns \((36 - 12) \times 12 = 288\), and joint profit is 576.

Nash equilibrium. A situation in which each economic actor, interacting with others, chooses their best strategy given the strategies all the other actors have chosen.

The cartel at 12 and 12 is not a Nash equilibrium, because a plant that deviates to 18 earns 324 against 288. The pair at 16 and 16 is one, since against a rival at 16 every deviation earns less than 256.

What the firms compete over. The same two plants, with the same demand curve and the same marginal cost, produce either the competitive outcome or one well above it. Which one depends only on what the firms choose to compete over, and neither outcome involves an agreement.

When Ridge and Vale choose quantities, they settle at 32 yards and $28, and each earns 256. When they post prices instead, they undercut each other down to $12, where the market takes 48 yards and each earns zero.

Game. A strategic situation described by three lists: the players, the strategies each may choose, and the payoff each receives for every combination of choices.

Mr Red and Mr Blue are questioned in separate rooms, and each may stay silent or confess. The payoffs are years in jail: one year each if both stay silent, two each if both confess, and zero for the one who confesses while the other stays silent and serves three.

Dominant strategy. A strategy that is best for a player no matter what strategy the other players choose.

Two food trucks each choose whether to keep their prices or to discount. If the rival keeps, a truck earns 650 by discounting against 500 by keeping, and if the rival discounts, it earns 400 against 300, so discounting is dominant.

Individual and collective rationality. Mutual silence gives one year each, which is better for Red and better for Blue than the two years each that rational play produces. So there is an outcome that is strictly better for both players at the same time, and rational play does not reach it. Individual rationality does not imply collective rationality.

Two streaming services that each choose a big advertising campaign end up with 80 each, although small campaigns would have given both of them 100.

Repeated game. The same game, played by the same players, round after round, with each round’s choices visible before the next.

Suppose Vale plays the grim strategy, mixing 16 forever once Ridge exceeds its quota. Cheating gains Ridge \(324 - 288 = 36\) once and costs it \(288 - 256 = 32\) in every later round, so if the plants expect to meet even twice more, \(36 < 32 + 32\) and cheating is a losing move.

The Sherman and Clayton Acts. The Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 declares illegal every contract, combination or conspiracy in restraint of trade. It makes it a crime to monopolize or attempt to monopolize, and it lets a private party injured by a violation sue for three times its losses. The Clayton Act of 1914 carries those treble damages forward and adds private injunctive relief, under which courts may order conduct stopped.

If Ridge and Vale agreed on output, that would be naked price fixing, illegal in itself. It would move the market from 32 yards at $28 toward 24 yards at $36.

Only \(Q\) is given. The price comes off the demand curve, total revenue is price times quantity, total cost is $12 a yard, and profit is the difference. Take the row for \(Q = 24\) as a check. The price is \(P = 60 - 24 = 36\). Total revenue is \(TR = 36 \times 24 = 864\). Total cost is \(TC = 12 \times 24 = 288\). So profit is \(864 - 288 = 576\).

The profit column starts at zero, rises to a peak of 576 at \(Q = 24\), falls back to zero at \(Q = 48\), and goes negative beyond that.

Where the sliding stops. Now try 16 yards each. Total output is \(Q = 32\), the price is \(60 - 32 = 28\), and each plant earns \((28 - 12) \times 16 = 256\).

The prisoners’ payoff matrix.

Blue stays silent Blue confesses
Red stays silent \((1,\;1)\) \((3,\;0)\)
Red confesses \((0,\;3)\) \((2,\;2)\)

Red picks the row and Blue picks the column. In every cell the first number is Red’s payoff and the second is Blue’s. The payoffs are years in jail, so fewer is better. The same ordering holds for every matrix in this chapter: the row player’s payoff comes first.

Reading the four cells in turn: if both stay silent, each serves one year on the minor charge. If Red stays silent and Blue confesses, Red serves three years while Blue walks. The reverse case is the mirror image. And if both confess, each serves two years.

The cartel as a payoff matrix.

Vale mixes 12 Vale mixes 16
Ridge mixes 12 \((288,\;288)\) \((240,\;320)\)
Ridge mixes 16 \((320,\;240)\) \((256,\;256)\)

Ridge’s profit comes first in each cell, and this time more is better. If Vale mixes 12, Ridge earns 320 by mixing 16, against 288 by staying at 12. If Vale mixes 16, Ridge earns 256 against 240. Mixing 16 wins both comparisons, so it is dominant, and by symmetry so is Vale’s.

Both plants mix 16, and the equilibrium is 256 each. Meanwhile 288 each is in plain view, preferred by both, and unreachable.

Four rounds with a known end. Ridge and Vale will interact for exactly four rounds, both of them know it, and Vale plays grim. Cooperating pays 288 a round, cheating against a rival at 12 pays 324, and the rounds after a cheat pay 256.

Cooperating throughout pays \(288 \times 4 = 1152\). Cheating in round one pays \(324 + 256 \times 3 = 324 + 768 = 1092\). That is worse, so cheating early loses. Cooperating in rounds one to three and then cheating in round four pays \(288 \times 3 + 324 = 864 + 324 = 1188\), which is the best of the three.

Cheating in the last round costs nothing, because there is no round left in which to be punished. So a known final round unravels cooperation at the end. What sustains real cartels is the absence of a fixed end.
