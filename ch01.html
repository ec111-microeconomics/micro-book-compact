1  Scarcity, Choice and the Frontier

1.1 Definitions

Scarcity. Scarcity means that resources are finite relative to what people want. Because resources are finite, there are competing claims on them. Scarcity means constraint. We cannot have more than there is, even if we want more.

Consider an economy with 6,000 hours of labor and no more. An hour that goes into a bicycle is an hour that cannot go into a jacket, and wanting more of both goods does not add a single hour to the supply.

Opportunity cost. An opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative that is forgone. It is measured in units of the thing that is given up, not necessarily in money. It is the only cost concept that appears in every model in this book.

Suppose a bicycle takes 6 hours of labor to make and a jacket takes 2. The opportunity cost of a bicycle is then 3 jackets, because that is what the 6 hours would otherwise have made.

Rationality. A rational agent is one who makes consistent choices given the objectives they have. They take the best action that serves their interests given all of the information and constraints that they face. Rational agents are in effect goal oriented.

Consider an airline about to depart with one unsold seat. If a standby passenger offers $300 for it, the airline takes the money, because $300 serves its interests better than a seat that flies empty.

Production possibility frontier. Given its technology, a production possibility frontier shows the maximum combinations of two goods that an economy can produce when all of its resources are used fully and efficiently.

Take the economy with 6,000 hours of labor, where a bicycle takes 6 hours and a jacket takes 2. Every hour is used on the frontier, so \(6B + 2J = 6000\), which rearranges to \(J = 3000 - 3B\). The frontier runs from 3,000 jackets and no bicycles to 1,000 bicycles and no jackets.

One picture, three ideas. The frontier brings together three ideas: scarcity, efficiency and opportunity cost. Scarcity shows up as the existence of a boundary, because some combinations are simply not available. Productive efficiency means being on the boundary rather than inside it. Opportunity cost is the slope of the boundary, leaving aside its minus sign. This is not an analogy. The size of the slope and the opportunity cost are one and the same number.

We can see all three on the frontier \(J = 3000 - 3B\). The combination of 400 bicycles and 1,800 jackets is on the boundary, so it is efficient, and the slope of \(-3\) says that one more bicycle costs 3 jackets. No point beyond the line can be reached with the hours available.

Constant opportunity cost gives a straight line. The equation \(J = 3000 - 3B\) has a slope of \(-3\), so each extra bicycle costs three jackets. That number is not something read off the graph. It comes straight from the labor data: \[\frac{6\ \text{hours per bicycle}}{2\ \text{hours per jacket}} = 3 \ \frac{\text{jackets}}{\text{bicycle}} .\] That is because the six hours that go into a bicycle could otherwise have made three jackets. Every hour is equally productive at either task, so the cost is the same at every point on the frontier. A constant slope gives a straight line.

Suppose the economy makes 400 bicycles and wants 100 more. At 3 jackets each, the extra bicycles cost 300 jackets, and jackets fall from 1,800 to 1,500.

Increasing opportunity cost. A straight-line frontier assumes that every unit of the resource is equally suited to making both goods. In practice, resources are usually specialized. So as production shifts toward one good, the expanding sector has to draw in resources that are less and less suited to it. Each additional unit therefore costs more than the one before. When the slope gets steeper, the opportunity cost increases and the frontier is bowed.

Suppose the frontier for food and clothing is \(C = 50 - 0.02F^{2}\). The first 10 units of food cost 2 units of clothing, and the last 10 units cost 18.

Three kinds of point. A point on the frontier is productively efficient: you cannot get more of one good without giving up some of the other. A point inside the frontier is feasible but inefficient, because the same resources could produce more of both goods. A point outside the frontier is out of reach with current resources and technology.

On the frontier \(C = 50 - 0.02F^{2}\), an economy that grows 40 units of food can make 18 units of clothing. So 40 food with 18 clothing is efficient, and 40 food with 24 clothing is out of reach. With 40 food and fewer than 18 clothing, the economy is inside its frontier.

Economic growth. Economic growth is an outward shift of the frontier. It enlarges the set of combinations that the economy can attain.

Go back to the point with 40 units of food and 24 of clothing, which the frontier \(C = 50 - 0.02F^{2}\) does not allow. Suppose that after growth the same 40 units of food permit about 32 units of clothing. The point has not moved, but it is now inside the frontier.

Positive and normative statements. A positive statement is a claim about how the world is, and in principle evidence can confirm or refute it. A normative statement is a claim about how the world ought to be. It rests on values as well as on facts.

The claim that a higher minimum wage reduces teenage employment is positive, because we can look at the data and the data may show it to be wrong. The claim that the government should raise the minimum wage is normative.

1.2 Worked examples

The cost of a year of college. An example illustrates the idea of an opportunity cost. Consider the cost of a year of college. Tuition and fees are the explicit costs. Let’s suppose these are $32,000. Forgone earnings are the implicit costs. For example, a year’s worth of full-time work might earn $38,000. Therefore the opportunity cost of the year is $70,000.

Notice that room and board don’t come into it, because whether you’re at college or working full time, you still have to pay for room and board. Only the difference between what they cost at college and what they would cost otherwise is part of the opportunity cost.

The concert ticket. As before, an example illustrates. Suppose you buy a concert ticket that costs $150. It is non-refundable, and you can’t sell it on to anybody else. On the night of the concert, it’s raining and you would rather stay in. No matter what, the $150 is gone. The reasonable comparison now is between the happiness, or what economists call utility, that you get from going to the concert and the utility you get from spending the evening at home. The price is now irrelevant.

A standard error is to suppose that not going to the concert now wastes $150. However, that is the wrong way to think about things. The $150 was already spent when the ticket was bought. The decision after the cost is sunk depends totally on the utility of the two options, staying at home or going to the concert. There is nothing you can do about the cost. Worrying about it, and thinking it should be part of the decision, is known as the sunk cost fallacy.

The empty seat. Once again, a simple example might illustrate this. Suppose an aircraft has 200 seats and is about to depart with one unsold seat. The average cost per passenger is $500. Now suppose there is a standby passenger, and that person offers $300. From the airline’s point of view, is the seat worth selling? The answer is yes, sell. Why? The flight is going to go regardless, so the crew, fuel, aircraft and everything else are already committed. The marginal cost of carrying one more passenger is near zero. It is a drink and a little more fuel, perhaps $5. So the marginal benefit to the airline is $300 against a marginal cost of a trivial $5.

The $500 average includes costs that do not change when the passenger boards. It answers a different question, and it is the wrong number when deciding what is the rational thing to do.

Tabulating the frontier.

Bicycles \(B\) Substitute Jackets \(J\)
0 \(3000-3(0)\) 3000
200 \(3000-3(200)\) 2400
400 \(3000-3(400)\) 1800
600 \(3000-3(600)\) 1200
800 \(3000-3(800)\) 600
1000 \(3000-3(1000)\) 0

Just as before, we can check one row directly against the constraint. At \(B=400\), \(J=1800\), and \(6(400) + 2(1800) = 2400 + 3600 = 6000\). So the bicycles and the jackets between them use up the hours exactly.

Read down the jackets column and each additional 200 bicycles costs exactly 600 jackets. The cost stays the same from row to row, and that constancy is what makes the line straight. Figure 1.1 plots the table and the line.
